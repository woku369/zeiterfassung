# Brew in a Bag (BIAB) All-Grain Brewing

**Confidence**: High
**Last validated**: 2025-01-27

Comprehensive guide to BIAB (Brew in a Bag) brewing -- the single-vessel, full-volume all-grain method that eliminates the traditional three-vessel setup. Covers equipment, water calculations, mash parameters, efficiency optimization, sparging options, limitations, and best practices.

---

## What Is BIAB?

Brew in a Bag (BIAB) is an all-grain brewing method that uses a single vessel and a large mesh bag to perform the entire mash and lauter process. Instead of the traditional three-vessel system (hot liquor tank, mash/lauter tun, boil kettle), BIAB combines all three functions into one kettle. The grain is contained in a mesh bag, mashed in the full volume of water needed for the batch, and then the bag is simply lifted out -- separating the wort from the grain in seconds rather than the 60+ minutes required for fly sparging.

### How BIAB Differs from Traditional Three-Vessel Brewing

| Aspect | Traditional 3-Vessel | BIAB |
|--------|---------------------|------|
| **Vessels required** | 3 (HLT, mash tun, boil kettle) | 1 (kettle + bag) |
| **Sparge** | Fly or batch sparge required | Optional (full-volume mash) |
| **Mash water** | Partial volume (~1.25-1.5 qt/lb) | Full volume (all water at once) |
| **Water-to-grain ratio** | 1.25-2.0 qt/lb | 2.5-4+ qt/lb (thinner mash) |
| **Lautering** | False bottom or manifold | Lift bag out |
| **Brew day length** | 5-7 hours | 3-5 hours |
| **Equipment cost** | $300-1000+ | $50-150 (kettle + bag) |
| **Grain crush** | Standard (0.035-0.045") | Finer recommended (0.025-0.035") |
| **Typical brewhouse efficiency** | 72-80% | 65-78% (no sparge), 75-85% (with sparge) |
| **Complexity** | Higher | Lower |
| **Space required** | Significant | Minimal |

### History and Origin

The concept of mashing grains in a bag dates back at least to Dave Line's *The Big Book of Brewing* (1974/1978), where Stage 2 of every recipe instructs: "Contain the mashed grain in a large grain bag to retrieve the sweet wort." Many British homebrewers were effectively doing BIAB in the 1970s-1980s without calling it that.

The **modern BIAB method** -- characterized by full-volume mashing in a single vessel with no required sparging -- was developed and refined on the **AussieHomeBrewer (AHB) forum** around 2006. Key figures:

- **Patrick "Pistol Patch" Hollingdale**: The primary advocate who refined, documented, and championed the method. He conducted extensive testing on full-volume mash efficiency, mash pH at full volume, crush size effects, and published comprehensive guides. He established biabrewer.info as a dedicated resource. Hollingdale fought for years to establish BIAB as a legitimate brewing method against skepticism from traditional all-grain brewers.
- **James Squire** (AHB forum member): Started the "All In One Brewery" thread on July 14, 2006, which inspired the gas-fired full-volume approach that became BIAB.
- **Ross**: Organized the first purpose-sewn BIAB bag for Hollingdale.

The Australian community's innovation was not just the bag itself, but the full-volume, no-sparge approach using a single vessel -- making all-grain brewing accessible to anyone with a pot and a bag. By 2011, the technique had spread to US forums (HomeBrewTalk's BIAB sub-forum), and by 2013-2014 it had become mainstream worldwide. The Australian community's consensus: "It wasn't invented here, but the method was certainly refined and improved here."

**Sources**: AussieHomeBrewer.com forums, HomeBrewTalk BIAB sub-forum, BeerSmith Podcast #10 (Pat Hollingdale interview), biabrewer.info.

## Equipment

### The Kettle

The kettle is the single most important piece of BIAB equipment. It must hold the **full volume** of water plus the displacement of the grain.

**Sizing rule of thumb**: Kettle volume should be approximately **2.5x your target batch size**.

| Batch Size | Minimum Kettle | Recommended Kettle | Max Grain Bill (approx.) |
|-----------|----------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| 2.5 gal | 5 gal | 5-6 gal | 6-8 lb |
| 3 gal | 5-6 gal | 8 gal | 8-10 lb |
| 5 gal | 8-10 gal | 10-12 gal | 12-16 lb |
| 10 gal | 15-20 gal | 20 gal | 24-30 lb |

**Material considerations**:
- **Stainless steel**: Preferred. Durable, easy to clean, no flavor impact. Can develop hot spots with direct fire -- stir to mitigate.
- **Aluminum**: Excellent heat transfer, lighter weight, less expensive. Some brewers prefer aluminum for its even heat distribution, which reduces scorching risk.
- **Kettle features**: A ball valve/spigot is helpful but not required. Volume markings are useful. A thermometer port is a convenience.

**Stovetop limitations**: Most household stovetops max out at 5-gallon kettles due to burner size and power. Stovetop brewers should plan on **2.5-3 gallon batches**. A conventional stove burner may struggle to bring 4+ gallons to a boil in a reasonable time.

### The Bag

The bag is the defining piece of BIAB equipment. It replaces the mash tun's false bottom or manifold.

**Requirements**:
- Must fit around the circumference of your kettle with enough overhang to secure at the rim
- Must be large enough to hold 10-30 lb of wet grain while submerged
- Must be fine enough mesh to retain grain particles while allowing wort to pass freely
- Must withstand sustained temperatures of 150-170F (65-77C) without degrading

**Materials**:

| Material | Mesh Size | Pros | Cons |
|----------|-----------|------|------|
| **Voile** (polyester sheer) | ~75-100 micron | Cheap ($5-10 for fabric), widely available, effective filtration | Less durable, may tear under heavy loads, DIY sewing required |
| **Nylon mesh** | 200-400 micron | Durable, good flow-through, widely available | Some debate about very fine particles passing through |
| **Polyester mesh (purpose-built)** | 200-400 micron | Strongest, purpose-sewn with reinforced seams, long-lasting | Most expensive ($25-50) |

**Micron ratings**:
- **200 micron**: Good general-purpose mesh. Adequate for most BIAB without recirculation.
- **400 micron**: Finer filtration. Recommended if you plan to recirculate your mash (e.g., in electric all-in-one systems). The finer mesh helps prevent particles from clogging the pump.

**Note on mesh rating**: In the BIAB context, a "400 micron" bag is described by some vendors as *finer* than 200 micron due to thread count differences. Always verify with the vendor. The important thing is that the mesh retains grain solids while flowing freely.

**Standard vs. custom bags**:
- **Standard bags**: Fit a range of kettle sizes, less expensive ($10-25). May bunch at the bottom or sides, which is fine for most brewing.
- **Custom bags**: Made to your exact kettle dimensions ($25-50, e.g., The Brew Bag). Can be sized to stay off the kettle bottom (important for direct-fire step mashing). Worth the investment for frequent BIAB brewers.

**Critical rule**: The bottom of the bag should not rest directly on the bottom of the kettle during the mash if you are applying direct heat. This prevents scorching the grain and potentially melting or damaging the bag. Use a false bottom, circular grill grate, or lift the bag slightly before applying heat.

### Heat Source

| Heat Source | Best For | Notes |
|-------------|----------|-------|
| **Propane burner** (outdoor) | 5-10 gal batches | Fast heating, high BTU. Banjo burners common. Best for large batches. |
| **Gas stovetop** | 2.5-3 gal batches | Convenient but limited heating power |
| **Electric stovetop** | 2.5 gal batches | Slowest heating, adequate for small batches |
| **Electric element (kettle-mounted)** | 5-10 gal batches | Precise temp control, indoor brewing. The basis of all-in-one electric systems. |
| **Induction burner** | 3-5 gal batches | Fast, precise, requires compatible kettle (magnetic stainless steel) |

### Pulley/Hoist System

For 5-gallon batches, the wet grain bag will weigh **25-45 lb** depending on the grain bill. A pulley system is strongly recommended for safety and convenience.

**Options**:
- **Rope and pulley**: Simplest and cheapest ($10-20). Mount to a sturdy overhead point (rafter, crossbeam). Functional but requires hand-over-hand lifting.
- **Ratchet pulley**: ($15-30). Locks at any height, making it easy to raise the bag in stages and hold it while it drains. Very popular choice among BIAB brewers.
- **Electric hoist**: ($100-150). Push-button operation, typically 250-440 lb capacity. Best for large batches or brewers with physical limitations.
- **A-frame/tripod**: Freestanding structure that goes over the kettle. No ceiling mount needed. Can be DIY or commercial ($40-100+).

**Mounting requirements**: Whatever you mount to must support **at least 150-250 lb**. Wet grain is heavy and the lifting force is significant. A rafter or crossbeam in a garage is ideal. An eye bolt rated for 150+ lb screwed into ceiling framing works. **Never mount to drywall alone.**

**Alternatives if no overhead mount**: Place a colander or large strainer over a bucket next to the kettle. Lift the bag into the colander and let it drain. Then add the drained wort back to the kettle. This works but is less elegant and loses some heat.

## Water Calculations

### The Full-Volume Concept

In traditional brewing, water is split between the mash (strike water) and sparge. In BIAB, **all the water goes into the mash at once** (full-volume mash). This is the simplest approach and eliminates the sparge step entirely.

### Calculating Total Water Needed

**Formula**:

```
Total Water = Batch Volume (into fermenter) + Boil-Off + Grain Absorption + Trub/Kettle Loss
```

**Typical values** (5-gallon batch example):

| Variable | Typical Value | Notes |
|----------|---------------|-------|
| Target batch volume | 5.0 gal | Into fermenter |
| Boil-off | 1.0-1.5 gal/hr | System dependent, measure yours |
| Boil time | 60 min | Standard (some recipes call for 90) |
| Grain absorption | 0.08-0.15 gal/lb | See absorption section below |
| Trub/kettle loss | 0.25-0.5 gal | Sediment left behind in kettle |

**Example calculation** (5-gallon batch, 10 lb grain bill, 60 min boil):

```
Batch volume:         5.00 gal
Boil-off (1.25 gal/hr x 1 hr): 1.25 gal
Grain absorption (10 lb x 0.10 gal/lb): 1.00 gal
Trub/kettle loss:     0.30 gal
-------------------------------
Total water needed:   7.55 gal
```

This 7.55 gallons is your strike water -- all goes into the kettle at once.

### Grain Absorption Rates

Grain absorption in BIAB is generally **lower** than in traditional mashing because the bag is lifted and the forces of gravity (and optionally squeezing) extract more liquid than a typical mash tun.

**Reported absorption rates for BIAB**:

| Method | Absorption Rate | Source |
|--------|----------------|--------|
| Gravity drain only (no squeeze) | 0.10-0.15 gal/lb | General homebrew consensus |
| Gravity drain + moderate squeeze | 0.06-0.10 gal/lb | HomeBrewTalk forums |
| biabcalculator.com default (no squeeze) | 0.045 gal/lb | biabcalculator.com |
| biabcalculator.com (with squeeze) | 0.031 gal/lb | biabcalculator.com |
| Traditional mash tun (for comparison) | 0.12-0.15 gal/lb | Standard brewing references |

**Note on the wide range**: The biabcalculator.com values (0.031-0.045 gal/lb) are significantly lower than the 0.10-0.15 gal/lb often cited on forums. This may reflect different measurement methodologies -- the calculator values may account for wort that drains out of the bag over a longer hang time plus squeezing, while forum values may reflect shorter drain times. The BeerSmith BIAB profile uses a grain absorption of 0.96 qt/lb (0.24 gal/lb) by default, which is closer to traditional values.

**Recommendation**: Start with **0.10 gal/lb** as a reasonable default if you plan to let the bag drip for 10+ minutes and give it a moderate squeeze. Adjust based on your actual measured losses over several brew days. Tracking your volumes is the most reliable path to dialing in your system.

### Strike Temperature

Because BIAB uses the full volume, the thermal mass of the water is much larger relative to the grain mass. This means the temperature drop when adding grain is smaller than in a traditional thick mash.

**Typical strike temperature**: 158-164F (70-73C) for a target mash of 148-156F (64-69C). The temperature will drop approximately **3-10F** when grain is added, depending on:
- Grain temperature (cold grain = more drop)
- Grain-to-water ratio (more grain relative to water = more drop)
- Ambient temperature

Use a brewing calculator (BeerSmith, Brewfather, biabcalculator.com) to get a precise strike temperature for your specific recipe and conditions. Or use this rough guideline:

```
Strike temp = Target mash temp + (0.2 x (Target mash temp - Grain temp) x (Grain weight / Water weight))
```

In practice, for BIAB's thin mash, adding **6-12F above target** to your strike water works for most situations. Overshoot slightly -- it is easier to add cold water or stir to cool than to reheat with the bag in place.

## Grain Crush

### Why Finer Crush Matters for BIAB

In traditional three-vessel brewing, a coarse crush is necessary to maintain a grain bed that allows wort to flow through without plugging (stuck sparge). BIAB has **no grain bed and no lauter** -- the bag does the filtering. This means BIAB brewers can crush significantly finer without any risk of a stuck sparge.

A finer crush exposes more starch surface area to enzymatic action, which means:
- **Faster starch conversion** (gelatinization proceeds from the surface of each particle inward; smaller particles convert faster)
- **Higher conversion efficiency** (more complete extraction of fermentable sugars)
- **More forgiveness with shorter mash times**

### Recommended Mill Gap Settings

| Setting | Gap Size | When to Use |
|---------|----------|-------------|
| Standard homebrew default | 0.039" (1.0 mm) | Traditional 3-vessel brewing |
| Fine BIAB crush (single pass) | 0.025-0.035" (0.6-0.9 mm) | Recommended for BIAB |
| Double crush at standard gap | Two passes at 0.039" | When you cannot adjust the mill (e.g., LHBS grinds for you) |
| Very fine / flour-like | <0.025" (< 0.6 mm) | Possible with BIAB but diminishing returns |

**Tip**: A credit card is approximately 0.030" (0.76 mm) thick. Setting your mill gap to a credit card's width produces a good BIAB crush in a single pass.

### Double Milling

If you cannot adjust your mill (common at local homebrew shops), double milling is a popular workaround: run the grain through the mill twice at the standard setting.

**Reported results**:
- Multiple brewers report efficiency gains of **10-15 percentage points** from double crushing (e.g., 69-75% jumping to 82-85%)
- Some experienced brewers find "no appreciable difference between a fine single crush and a standard double crush" -- suggesting that a single pass at the right gap setting is preferable to double-crushing at a wide gap
- One experienced BIAB brewer sets a two-roller mill gap to **0.016"** and achieves consistently high efficiency with a single pass

**Practical recommendation**: If you own a mill, set the gap to 0.025-0.035" for BIAB. If buying pre-crushed grain, ask for a "fine crush" or "BIAB crush" and consider double-milling if the shop cannot adjust their gap.

### Concerns with Very Fine Crush

- **Flour settling and scorching**: Very fine particles can settle to the bottom of the kettle and scorch when heat is applied. Stir well and consider a false bottom or raised bag.
- **Astringency**: Not caused by the crush itself. Astringency results from pH above 5.8 or temperatures above 170F (77C). Keep your mash pH in the 5.2-5.6 range and you will not get astringency from a fine crush.
- **Diminishing returns**: At some point, crushing finer yields no additional efficiency gain. Most brewers find the sweet spot at "coarse cornmeal" consistency.

**Sources**: HomeBrewTalk forums, Brewer's Friend BIAB crush experiments, Brulosophy grain crush exBEERiments, BeerAdvocate BIAB threads.

## Mash Parameters

### Water-to-Grain Ratio

BIAB produces a **much thinner mash** than traditional brewing because all the water is present at once:

| Method | Typical Ratio | Notes |
|--------|--------------|-------|
| Traditional (batch sparge) | 1.25-1.5 qt/lb | Thick mash |
| Traditional (fly sparge) | 1.25-2.0 qt/lb | Moderate |
| BIAB (full volume) | 2.5-4.0+ qt/lb | Very thin mash |

In BIAB, you do not typically choose your water-to-grain ratio -- it is a consequence of your batch size, boil-off, and grain bill. "It just is what it is." A 5-gallon batch with 10 lb of grain and 7.5 gallons of strike water yields approximately **3.0 qt/lb** -- roughly double the traditional mash thickness.

### Effects of Thin Mash on Conversion

Research findings on thin mash:

**Briggs (2004, *Brewing Science in Practice*):**
- Extract efficiency at 2 L/kg (1 qt/lb): 71.7%
- Extract efficiency at 4 L/kg (2 qt/lb): 80.0%
- Extract efficiency at 5 L/kg (2.4 qt/lb): 80.0% (no further improvement)
- Conclusion: "A concentrated mash actually inhibits starch conversion." Most conversion potential is reached by 2.5 L/kg.

**Troester (2008, Braukaiser.com):**
- Thick mash (2.57 L/kg): 58-60% brewhouse efficiency, stable across temperatures
- Thin mash (5 L/kg): 68% efficiency peak -- nearly 8% higher than thick
- Thin mash efficiency showed strong temperature dependency (more responsive to mash temp changes)
- **No measurable difference in attenuation** between thin and thick mashes at the same temperature -- debunking the old claim that thin mashes produce "thinner beer"

**Why thin mash works**: Modern highly-modified malts have enzyme content well in excess of the minimum needed for starch conversion. Even at the lower enzyme concentration of a thin mash, there is more than enough diastatic power for complete conversion. Furthermore, the full water volume is in contact with the full grain mass for the entire mash duration -- unlike a sparge where water passes through quickly.

### Temperature Targets

Standard BIAB mash temperatures are the same as any all-grain brew:

| Mash Type | Temperature | Purpose |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Low (beta amylase favored) | 148-150F (64-66C) | More fermentable wort, drier/thinner body, higher attenuation |
| Medium (balanced) | 152-154F (67-68C) | Balance of fermentability and body. Good default. |
| High (alpha amylase favored) | 156-158F (69-70C) | Less fermentable, fuller body, sweeter finish |
| Mash-out | 168-170F (76-77C) | Denatures enzymes, improves flow and sugar extraction |

**Default BIAB mash temperature**: 152-153F (67C) is the most common starting point, providing a good balance.

**Note on thin mash and fermentability**: While the traditional brewing wisdom says thin mashes favor beta amylase (producing more fermentable wort), Troester's experiments found no measurable difference in attenuation between thin and standard mash thicknesses. Mash temperature remains the primary lever for controlling fermentability. BIAB does not inherently produce thinner-bodied beers.

### Mash Time

| Duration | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| 60 minutes | Standard, adequate for well-modified base malts with a good crush |
| 75 minutes | Common BIAB recommendation for improved efficiency |
| 90 minutes | Aggressive target; frequently recommended for BIAB to maximize conversion |

Many BIAB brewers extend the mash to 75-90 minutes to compensate for the thinner mash and to ensure complete conversion, particularly with coarser crushes. With a fine crush and good temperature control, 60 minutes is usually sufficient.

### Mash-Out

A mash-out step (raising temperature to 168-170F / 76-77C and holding for 10 minutes) is optional but can help:
- Denature remaining enzymes, locking in the sugar profile
- Reduce wort viscosity, allowing it to drain more freely from the grain bag
- Improve extraction efficiency slightly

**How to mash out in BIAB**: With the bag still in the kettle, apply heat (direct fire or electric element) while stirring gently. **Lift the bag slightly off the bottom** if using direct fire to prevent scorching. Hold at 168-170F for 10 minutes, then remove the bag.

### pH Management

BIAB's thinner mash results in a **slightly higher mash pH** compared to a traditional thick mash with the same grain bill and water. This is because the grain's buffering capacity is more diluted.

- Target mash pH: **5.2-5.6** (measured at room temperature on a cooled sample)
- If your water has high alkalinity (>100 ppm as CaCO3), pH adjustment is especially important for BIAB
- Common adjustments: Lactic acid (a few mL per gallon), phosphoric acid, acidulated malt (1-3% of grain bill)
- See the [Water Chemistry module](water-chemistry.md) for detailed guidance

## The Squeeze Debate

### The Myth: "Don't Squeeze -- It Extracts Tannins"

This is one of the most persistent myths in homebrewing, and it has been **thoroughly debunked** by both experimental evidence and brewing science.

### What the Science Says

Tannin extraction is caused by **two factors**:
1. **High pH** (above 5.8-6.0 in the mash)
2. **High temperature** (above 170F / 77C)

Physical pressure (squeezing) does **not** extract tannins. The chemistry is clear: tannins are polyphenols bound in the grain husk. They become soluble under high pH and high temperature conditions. Applying mechanical pressure to drain liquid does not change the pH or temperature, and therefore does not change what compounds are in solution.

### Experimental Evidence

**Brulosophy exBEERiment** (2017): Conducted a side-by-side comparison of squeezed vs. unsqueezed BIAB batches. Tasters could not reliably distinguish between the two beers. Neither beer exhibited astringency.

**Commercial practice**: Multiple commercial breweries use mash filter systems with hydraulic or pneumatic presses that apply far more pressure than any human could with a bag. Alaskan Brewing is one well-known example. These breweries produce clean, non-astringent beer while squeezing the grain with industrial force -- because they control their pH and temperature.

### The Community Consensus

Across HomeBrewTalk, AussieHomeBrewer, Reddit r/homebrewing, BeerAdvocate, and Brewer's Friend forums, the consensus is clear:

- **Squeeze the bag.** Extract every drop of wort.
- John Palmer's advice: "Squeeze -- leave no wort behind."
- As long as mash pH is below 5.8 and temperature is below 170F, squeezing is beneficial.
- Squeezing can improve lauter efficiency by **5-10%** -- that is significant.

**One nuance**: John Palmer has noted that for very dark beers (stouts, porters), aggressive squeezing may extract more harsh compounds from roasted grains. This is a different mechanism than tannin extraction and may be worth considering for heavily roasted grain bills. However, most BIAB brewers squeeze all beers without issue.

### Practical Approach

1. After mashing, lift the bag and let it drain by gravity for 5-10 minutes (use a pulley, colander, or helper)
2. Once initial draining slows, squeeze the bag with moderate to firm pressure
3. Use heat-resistant gloves or let the bag cool slightly before squeezing
4. Collect all the squeezed wort -- it is all good sugary wort
5. If you are paranoid, taste the last squeezings. They will not taste astringent.

**Sources**: Brulosophy exBEERiment (May 2017), The Brew Bag misconceptions blog, HomeBrewTalk squeeze threads (177051, 693765), BeerSmith blog, Palmer's *How to Brew*.

## Efficiency Expectations

### Types of Efficiency (Important Distinction)

Efficiency discussions are confusing because people mean different things:

| Efficiency Type | What It Measures | Typical Range |
|----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Conversion/mash efficiency** | How completely starches convert to sugars in the mash | 80-95% |
| **Lauter efficiency** | How much sugar you extract from the grain bed | 70-90% |
| **Pre-boil efficiency** | Sugar in kettle pre-boil vs. theoretical max | 65-85% |
| **Brewhouse efficiency** | Sugar in fermenter vs. theoretical max | 60-80% |

When BIAB brewers discuss "efficiency," they usually mean **brewhouse efficiency** -- the total sugar in the fermenter compared to the theoretical potential of the grain.

### Typical BIAB Brewhouse Efficiency

| Approach | Typical Range | Notes |
|----------|--------------|-------|
| Full-volume, no sparge, standard crush | 55-68% | First-time BIAB brewers, shop-crushed grain |
| Full-volume, no sparge, fine crush, squeeze | 68-78% | Most experienced BIAB brewers land here |
| With dunk or pour-over sparge | 75-85% | Adding a simple sparge step boosts efficiency significantly |
| Optimized (fine crush + sparge + pH control + extended mash) | 80-88% | Dialed-in systems |

**Key data points from the community**:
- Brew Your Own magazine reports 74-84% for OGs of 1.040-1.075
- Rex Slagel tracked 78.5% average extract efficiency across 150+ BIAB batches (BYO)
- One study of 33 BIAB batches showed 77.5% average pre-boil efficiency
- HomeBrewTalk consensus: 70-75% is typical for full-volume no-sparge
- AussieHomeBrewer consensus: experienced BIAB brewers regularly hit 75-82%
- Efficiency decreases as gravity increases (larger grain bills relative to water)

### How to Improve BIAB Efficiency

Ranked roughly by impact:

1. **Crush finer** (biggest single improvement): Set mill gap to 0.025-0.035" or double-crush. Many brewers report 10-15 point improvements from crush alone.

2. **Add a sparge step**: Even a simple dunk sparge can add 5-15 percentage points. See the Sparging section below.

3. **Control mash pH**: Target 5.2-5.6. Correct pH dramatically improves enzyme activity and extraction. This is often overlooked by beginning BIAB brewers.

4. **Squeeze the bag thoroughly**: Recovers 0.5-1.0+ gallons of wort that would otherwise be lost in the grain.

5. **Extend mash time**: Go to 75-90 minutes instead of 60, especially with coarser crushes.

6. **Stir the mash**: Stir every 15-20 minutes during the mash to ensure even extraction and prevent clumping.

7. **Mash out**: Raise to 168-170F for 10 minutes before removing the bag. Reduces viscosity and improves drainage.

8. **Calibrate your system**: Know your actual boil-off rate, actual grain absorption, and actual trub loss. Inaccurate system parameters in your brewing software can make efficiency appear lower than it actually is.

### When Not to Chase Efficiency

Many experienced BIAB brewers adopt a pragmatic approach: set your brewing software to assume **70-72% efficiency**, build grain bills accordingly, and stop worrying. The cost of a little extra grain (maybe $0.50-1.00 per batch) is trivial compared to the time spent chasing every efficiency point. **Consistency matters more than a high number.**

## Sparge Options

### Option 1: No Sparge (Full-Volume BIAB) -- Simplest

This is "pure" BIAB as originally developed in Australia.

**Process**:
1. Calculate total water needed (batch volume + boil-off + grain absorption + trub loss)
2. Heat all water to strike temperature
3. Add grain bag and grain, stir, mash for 60-90 minutes
4. Lift bag, drain, squeeze
5. Proceed to boil

**Pros**: Simplest process, fewest vessels, fastest brew day, no risk of over-sparging or pH issues
**Cons**: Lower efficiency (65-78% typical), requires largest kettle, highest grain use

**Best for**: Brewers prioritizing simplicity, low-to-moderate gravity beers (OG < 1.065), large kettle owners

### Option 2: Dunk Sparge -- Most Popular Sparge Method

A dunk sparge rinses additional sugars from the grain by submerging the bag in a second vessel of hot water after the mash.

**Process**:
1. Reserve 1.0-2.0 gallons from your total water volume. Heat this reserved water to 168-170F (76-77C) in a separate pot.
2. Mash as normal with the remaining water in the main kettle.
3. After the mash, lift the bag from the main kettle and transfer it to the pot of hot sparge water.
4. Submerge, stir or dunk like a tea bag for 5-15 minutes.
5. Lift bag, drain, squeeze.
6. Add the sparge water (now enriched with rinsed sugars) to the main kettle.
7. Proceed to boil.

**Efficiency gain**: Typically +5-15 percentage points over no-sparge. One brewer reported jumping from 63% to 87% with a dunk sparge (though this likely also reflected other process improvements).

**Time added**: 5-10 minutes. The dunk can happen while your main kettle is heating toward boil, adding virtually no time.

**Equipment needed**: A second pot (4-8 gallon) or insulated cooler

**Tip**: The sparge water should be at or below 170F (77C). Above this temperature, tannin extraction risk increases.

### Option 3: Pour-Over Sparge

Hot water is poured over the suspended grain bag, rinsing sugars as it passes through -- similar in concept to fly sparging.

**Process**:
1. Reserve 1.0-2.0 gallons of water, heat to 168-170F (76-77C).
2. After the mash, lift the bag and suspend it above the kettle (pulley, A-frame, or helper holding it).
3. Slowly pour the hot sparge water over the top of the grain bag, allowing it to percolate through the grain and drip into the kettle below.
4. Pour slowly and evenly to maximize contact time.
5. Once all sparge water has passed through, squeeze the bag and proceed to boil.

**Efficiency gain**: Similar to dunk sparge (+5-15 points)

**Challenges**: Pouring hot water over a suspended bag can be messy and awkward. Some brewers build DIY pour-over rigs (e.g., a bucket with small holes in the bottom positioned above the bag for a slow, even rinse).

**Advanced variation -- trickle sparge**: One brewer made a perforated bucket (Home Depot bucket with holes drilled in the bottom, calibrated to release 8 quarts over 30 minutes) placed above the suspended grain bag. This mimics fly sparging and achieved 80-82% efficiency.

### Choosing a Sparge Approach

| Factor | No Sparge | Dunk Sparge | Pour-Over |
|--------|-----------|-------------|-----------|
| Simplicity | Best | Good | Fair |
| Equipment | 1 vessel | 2 vessels | 1 vessel + pulley |
| Time added | None | 5-10 min | 10-30 min |
| Efficiency | 65-78% | 75-85% | 75-85% |
| Messiness | Lowest | Low | Higher |
| Best for | Simplicity | Balance of simplicity and efficiency | Efficiency focus |

## Limitations and Considerations

### Kettle Volume Constraints

This is the primary limitation of BIAB. You need enough kettle volume for:
- All your water
- All your grain (grain displaces roughly 0.08 gal per pound)
- Headroom to avoid overflow

**Practical grain bill limits by kettle size** (5-gallon batch, no sparge):

| Kettle Size | Max Grain Bill (approx.) | Max OG (approx.) |
|-------------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| 8 gal | 10-12 lb | 1.050-1.060 |
| 10 gal | 14-16 lb | 1.065-1.075 |
| 12 gal | 18-20 lb | 1.080-1.090 |
| 15 gal | 24-26 lb | 1.095-1.110 |

### High-Gravity Beers

Brewing high-gravity beers (OG > 1.075) with BIAB presents challenges:
- Large grain bills may not fit in the kettle with full water volume
- Grain absorption takes a larger share of the total water
- Efficiency drops as gravity increases (less water per pound of grain)

**Workarounds for high-gravity BIAB**:

1. **Sparge** (most effective): Mash with less water (thicker mash), then sparge to recover sugars. This is the most common approach and works well up to 1.090+.

2. **Double mash**: Mash a first batch of grain, remove the bag, then mash a second batch of grain in the same wort. Some brewers have produced 1.100+ beers this way. One brewer made a 1.105 barleywine by mashing two separate grain charges in sequence.

3. **Supplement with extract or sugar**: Use the grain bill for flavor and character, then add dry malt extract (DME) or simple sugars to hit target gravity. Belgian styles especially benefit from this approach, as sugar adjuncts are traditional.

4. **Extended boil**: A longer boil (90-120 minutes) concentrates the wort. Czech breweries historically used 2-hour boils. This adds time but no additional complexity.

5. **Post-boil dilution in reverse**: Brew a concentrated wort (smaller batch volume, higher gravity) and do not dilute. Accept a smaller batch at higher gravity.

6. **Larger kettle**: The simplest permanent solution. A 15-20 gallon kettle opens up virtually any grain bill.

### Batch Size Limits

BIAB scales from small (1-gallon stovetop) to large (10+ gallon with appropriate equipment). For very large batches:
- The bag weight becomes extreme (40-60+ lb of wet grain for a 10-gallon batch)
- An electric hoist or two-person lift becomes necessary
- Some brewers split the grain between two bags for easier handling
- Purpose-built BIAB systems (Clawhammer, etc.) handle 10-20 gallon batches with built-in baskets

### Wort Clarity

BIAB wort is often cloudier than wort from a traditional lauter tun, especially with a fine crush. This is generally **not a problem**:
- The boil, hot break, and cold break will remove much of the haze
- Whirlfloc or Irish moss added in the last 10-15 minutes of the boil helps
- Cold crashing and fining agents (gelatin) work as well as ever
- Research (referenced in BYO) suggests turbid wort may actually produce better fermentation and flavor
- The beer will clear with normal conditioning

## Tips and Best Practices

### Before Brew Day

- **Condition your new bag**: Wash a new bag in hot water before first use to remove any manufacturing residues. No detergent needed -- just hot water.
- **Pre-heat the bag**: Some brewers soak the bag in the heating strike water for a few minutes before adding grain. This ensures the bag is fully pliable and at temperature.
- **Pre-heat your grain** (optional): Bringing grain to room temperature (if stored cold) reduces the temperature drop at mash-in.
- **Use a brewing calculator**: BeerSmith, Brewfather, biabcalculator.com, or the Priceless BIAB Calculator. Set your system profile for BIAB-specific grain absorption and efficiency.
- **Plan for grain weight**: Know your grain bill and make sure your lifting setup (pulley, helper, muscles) can handle it wet.

### During the Mash

- **Secure the bag**: Drape the bag over the rim of the kettle and secure with clips, bungee cords, or clothespins. The bag must not slip into the kettle.
- **Stir well at mash-in**: Break up all dough balls. Dough balls are pockets of dry grain that will not convert.
- **Stir periodically**: Every 15-20 minutes, give the mash a gentle stir. This improves extraction and ensures even temperature.
- **Insulate the kettle**: Wrap the kettle in a sleeping bag, blanket, towel, or purpose-made insulation jacket during the mash. This maintains temperature stability, especially in cold weather. Most BIAB brewers lose 2-5F over a 60-minute mash with insulation, which is acceptable.
- **Do not open the lid unnecessarily**: Every time you open the lid, you lose heat. Set a timer and walk away.
- **If applying heat during mash** (step mashing or temperature correction): Lift the bag off the bottom of the kettle first, or use a false bottom/rack. Stir constantly while heating. Direct heat on a stationary bag will scorch the grain and potentially melt the bag.

### Lifting and Draining the Bag

- **Use heat-resistant gloves**: The bag is hot (150F+). Silicone or heavy rubber gloves protect your hands.
- **Lift slowly**: Let wort drain gradually. Rushing increases mess and can tear the bag.
- **Let it drip for 5-10+ minutes**: Patience here is free efficiency.
- **Then squeeze**: See the Squeeze section above. Use gloves, squeeze firmly, get that wort.
- **Have a plan for spent grain**: A bucket, trash bag, or compost bin. Wet spent grain is heavy and messy. Many brewers compost it, give it to farmers for animal feed, or use it in baking (spent grain bread).

### During the Boil

- **Watch for boil-overs**: The transition from heating to boiling (hot break) is when boil-overs are most likely. Watch the kettle closely as it approaches boiling. The hot break foam can rise extremely fast.
- **Reduce heat once boiling**: You want a rolling boil, not a volcanic eruption. A vigorous but controlled boil is sufficient.
- **Spray bottle trick**: Keep a spray bottle of cold water nearby. A quick spray knocks down rising foam instantly.
- **Fermcap-S**: A silicone-based anti-foam agent (a few drops) that virtually eliminates boil-overs. Safe, effective, widely used. Particularly helpful for BIAB since the full-volume boil has more headspace constraints.

### Cleaning

The BIAB bag does **not** need to be sanitized or sterilized between uses -- everything is boiled during the mash and boil anyway.

**Cleaning methods** (choose one):
- **Simple rinse**: Turn inside out, rinse with hot water, shake out loose grain, hang to dry
- **Hose down**: Spray thoroughly with a garden hose after dumping spent grain
- **Washing machine**: Rinse in sink first, then wash on gentle cycle with very light detergent (no fabric softener). Hang to dry.
- **OxiClean/PBW soak**: For deep cleaning, soak in OxiClean or PBW solution for an hour, then rinse thoroughly

**Critical**: Do not let the bag sit wet with grain in it for days -- mold will grow. Clean promptly or at least rinse and hang to dry on brew day.

**Bag lifespan**: A quality polyester or nylon BIAB bag (e.g., The Brew Bag) should last 50-100+ brews with proper care. Voile bags may last 10-30 brews before developing tears.

## BIAB with Electric All-in-One Systems

Many modern electric brewing systems (Grainfather, Anvil Foundry, Spike Solo, Clawhammer, Mash & Boil) are essentially **BIAB systems with built-in baskets and heating elements**. They share the same single-vessel principle:

- Grain goes into a perforated basket (the "bag equivalent")
- Full-volume or near-full-volume mash in one vessel
- Basket is lifted out after mashing
- Boil happens in the same vessel

The principles in this guide apply to these systems with minor adjustments:
- Use the system's recommended grain basket gap or mesh, not a fabric bag
- Recirculation pumps change the efficiency equation (generally higher efficiency)
- Follow the manufacturer's water calculation guidance, which accounts for their specific dead space and losses
- These systems often have built-in temperature control, eliminating the need for insulation

See the [Equipment Guide](../resources/equipment-guide.md) for detailed reviews of specific electric systems.

## Quick Reference: BIAB at a Glance

**Minimum equipment**: Kettle (2.5x batch size), mesh bag, heat source, thermometer, stirring spoon

**Water calculation**: Batch volume + boil-off + grain absorption (0.10 gal/lb default) + trub loss = total water

**Strike temperature**: Target mash temp + 6-12F (varies; use calculator)

**Default mash**: 152-153F for 60-90 minutes

**Crush**: Finer than traditional; 0.025-0.035" gap or double-crush

**Squeeze the bag**: Yes. The tannin myth is debunked.

**Typical efficiency**: 68-78% brewhouse (no sparge), 75-85% (with sparge)

**Simplest sparge**: Dunk sparge in 1.5 gal of 170F water, adds 5-10 min to brew day

**Mash pH target**: 5.2-5.6

**Key calculators**: biabcalculator.com, BeerSmith, Brewfather, Priceless BIAB Calculator
