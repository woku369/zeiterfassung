# Country Wine Production

Country wines—also called fruit wines, flower wines, or non-grape wines—are fermented beverages made from any sugar-containing plant material other than grapes. This tradition spans cultures and millennia, from ancient mead to British hedgerow wines to tropical fruit wines worldwide.

## Fundamental Principles

### What Distinguishes Country Wines from Grape Wines

Grape wine benefits from a near-perfect natural balance of sugar, acid, tannin, and nutrients. Country wines require the winemaker to engineer this balance:

1. **Sugar adjustment**: Most fruits lack sufficient sugar for wine-strength alcohol
2. **Acid balancing**: Some fruits are too acidic, others too bland
3. **Body building**: Many fruits lack the tannins that give wine structure
4. **Nutrient supplementation**: Non-grape musts often lack adequate yeast nutrients

### The Basic Country Wine Method

The traditional British method, refined over centuries:

1. **Prepare the fruit**: Wash, chop, crush, or press depending on the ingredient
2. **Extract flavor**: Either by boiling (vegetables, grains) or steeping in cold/warm water (delicate fruits, flowers)
3. **Create the must**: Add sugar, acid (citrus juice), and body-builders (raisins, grape concentrate, or tea for tannin)
4. **Pitch yeast**: After cooling to appropriate temperature
5. **Primary fermentation**: In open or loosely covered vessel, 5-7 days with daily stirring
6. **Strain and rack**: Transfer to secondary vessel under airlock
7. **Secondary fermentation**: Until fermentation ceases (weeks to months)
8. **Rack periodically**: Every 2-3 months to clarify
9. **Bottle**: When clear and stable
10. **Age**: Most country wines benefit from 6-12 months aging

### Critical Additives

**Raisins**: The traditional body-builder. Add 8-16 oz per gallon to provide:
- Vinous character and depth
- Natural tannins
- Trace nutrients for yeast health
- Fermentable sugars

**Citric acid or citrus juice**: Most country wines need acid adjustment
- 2-3 lemons or oranges per gallon is standard
- Use juice only (pith adds bitterness)
- Target pH 3.2-3.5 for stability and brightness

**Pectin enzyme (Pectolase)**: Essential for fruit wines
- Breaks down pectin that causes haze
- Improves juice extraction
- Add before fermentation, not after (alcohol inhibits enzyme action)

**Tannin**: For structure and aging potential
- Cold tea (1 cup strong tea per gallon)
- Grape tannin powder
- Oak chips during secondary

**Yeast nutrient**: Country wines often lack nitrogen
- Commercial nutrient blends
- Or add 1/4 tsp yeast nutrient per gallon

## Fruit Wines by Category

### Stone Fruits (Apricot, Cherry, Damson, Greengage, Plum, Sloe)

Stone fruits make exceptional wines with complex flavor profiles. The challenge is pectin content and potential for excessive tannin from skins and stones.

**General approach**:
- Remove stones (contains trace cyanogenic compounds; a few cracked for almond notes is traditional)
- Use pectin enzyme generously
- Shorter maceration (3-5 days) to avoid excessive tannin extraction
- Expect 3-4 lb fruit per gallon

**Apricot Wine**
One of the finest country wines, with delicate floral aromatics.

```
4 lb ripe apricots, halved and stoned
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins, chopped
Juice of 2 lemons
1 tsp pectin enzyme
1 tsp yeast nutrient
Wine yeast (Lalvin EC-1118 or similar)
Water to 1 gallon
```

Pour boiling water over apricots and raisins. When cool, add pectin enzyme. After 24 hours, add remaining ingredients except yeast. Add yeast when at room temperature. Ferment on pulp 5 days, stirring daily. Strain, transfer to secondary. OG ~1.090.

**Cherry Wine**
Use sour cherries (Morello type) for best results; sweet cherries make bland wine.

```
4 lb sour cherries, stemmed
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
1 tsp pectin enzyme
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Crush cherries lightly (do not break stones). Add boiling water. Proceed as for apricot wine. The color is magnificent—deep ruby.

**Damson Wine**
A traditional English favorite with intense plum flavor and natural acidity.

```
4 lb damsons
3 lb sugar (damsons are tart)
8 oz raisins
1 tsp pectin enzyme
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Pour boiling water over damsons. When cool enough to handle, squeeze each damson to release flesh (or freeze and thaw first to break down cell walls). Ferment on pulp 5 days. This wine improves dramatically with 2+ years aging.

**Sloe Wine**
From blackthorn berries, traditionally gathered after first frost.

```
4 lb sloes (after frost or frozen)
3 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 1 lemon
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Prick each sloe or freeze/thaw to break skins. Pour boiling water over fruit. Ferment 7 days on pulp. Produces a dry, tannic wine that ages superbly—5+ years ideal.

### Berries (Blackberry, Blackcurrant, Elderberry, Gooseberry, Raspberry, Strawberry)

Berries produce some of the most popular and successful country wines. High in color, flavor, and often acid.

**Blackberry Wine**
The classic hedgerow wine, deep purple and full-bodied.

```
4 lb ripe blackberries
2.5 lb sugar
Juice of 2 lemons
1 tsp pectin enzyme
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Crush berries in fermentation vessel. Add sugar dissolved in warm water, lemon juice, pectin enzyme. Add yeast when cool. Ferment on pulp 5 days, pressing gently to extract color. One of the easiest and most reliable country wines.

**Elderberry Wine**
Called "the Englishman's grape" for its wine-like qualities. Rich, port-like, excellent aging potential.

```
3 lb elderberries (stripped from stems)
3 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 1 lemon
1/2 oz root ginger, bruised
6 cloves
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Elderberries are intensely flavored—3 lb is sufficient. Boil berries with ginger and cloves for 15 minutes (elderberries should be heated to destroy sambunigrin). Strain onto sugar and raisins. Ferment when cool. This wine peaks at 3-5 years.

**Elderberry Wine (Dry Style)**
```
3 lb elderberries
2 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast (Lalvin RC-212 for Burgundy style)
Water to 1 gallon
```

Less sugar produces a drier, more table-wine-like result. Still needs 2+ years aging.

**Blackcurrant Wine**
Intensely flavored, high in vitamin C, makes excellent cordials and dessert wines.

```
3 lb blackcurrants
3 lb sugar
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Crush fruit, add boiling water, steep 3 days. High natural acidity—no lemon needed. Best as a sweet wine (add sugar to 1.100 OG).

**Gooseberry Wine**
Often called the best white country wine, resembling Sauvignon Blanc when well made.

```
4 lb gooseberries (green, slightly underripe)
2.5 lb sugar
Juice of 2 lemons
1 tsp pectin enzyme
Wine yeast (Lalvin EC-1118)
Water to 1 gallon
```

Top, tail, and crush gooseberries. Add cold water and pectin enzyme (heat destroys delicate aromatics). Ferment on pulp 5 days. Produces a crisp, aromatic white wine.

**Raspberry Wine**
Aromatic and delicate, one of the most refined fruit wines.

```
3.5 lb raspberries
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz white grape juice concentrate
1 tsp pectin enzyme
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Crush raspberries gently. Add cold water and pectin enzyme. After 24 hours, add sugar and concentrate. Ferment on pulp 3-4 days only (longer extracts too much tannin from seeds). Strain carefully. Ready younger than most country wines—6-12 months.

**Strawberry Wine**
Beautiful color but notoriously difficult—delicate flavor easily lost.

```
4 lb strawberries, hulled
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz white grape juice concentrate
Juice of 2 lemons
1 tsp pectin enzyme
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Crush strawberries and add cold water. Add pectin enzyme; wait 24 hours. Add remaining ingredients. Ferment on pulp only 3 days. Handle gently throughout—strawberry wine oxidizes easily. Drink young.

### Pome Fruits (Apple, Pear, Quince)

Apples and pears make true ciders and perries when fermented alone; as "wine" they're usually supplemented.

**Apple Wine**
Distinct from cider in that sugar is added to increase alcohol.

```
1 gallon fresh apple juice (pressed or purchased)
1 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 1 lemon
Wine yeast
```

Simple method: add sugar and lemon to apple juice, pitch yeast. For more body, include raisins in primary. Target OG 1.085-1.095. See also cider recipes in resources/recipes.md for pure cider approach.

**Pear Wine**
Similar to apple wine; pears ferment well but lack acidity.

```
1 gallon fresh pear juice
1.5 lb sugar
Juice of 3 lemons (pears need more acid)
1 tsp tannin powder
Wine yeast
```

Pears oxidize rapidly—add lemon juice immediately after pressing.

**Quince Wine**
Aromatic and unusual; quinces must be cooked as they're inedible raw.

```
3 lb quinces, chopped (including cores)
2.5 lb sugar
Juice of 2 lemons
1 tsp pectin enzyme
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Boil quinces until soft (30-40 minutes). Strain, add sugar while warm. When cool, add remaining ingredients. Produces a golden, perfumed wine.

### Citrus Wines

Citrus wines are sharp and refreshing, best as aperitifs or for blending.

**Orange Wine**
Seville (bitter) oranges make the best wine; sweet oranges work but are less complex.

```
12 oranges (preferably Seville)
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
1 tsp pectin enzyme
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Peel oranges thinly (no white pith). Juice the oranges. Pour boiling water over peels and raisins. When cool, add juice, lemon juice, and sugar. Ferment on peels 5 days, then strain. Golden color, marmalade aromatics.

**Lemon Wine**
Intensely acidic; dilute well and use plenty of sugar.

```
8 lemons
3 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
1 cup cold strong tea (for tannin)
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Peel lemons thinly. Juice them. Proceed as for orange wine. Best as a sweet wine.

### Tropical Fruits

Tropical fruit wines are popular in their regions of origin and increasingly worldwide.

**Banana Wine**
Surprisingly good; very full-bodied.

```
4 lb ripe bananas (with brown spots)
2.5 lb sugar
Juice of 2 lemons
1 cup cold strong tea
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Slice bananas (skins can be included for more body). Boil in water 30 minutes. Strain onto sugar. Add remaining ingredients when cool. Ferments slowly; produces a full, sweet wine.

**Mango Wine**
Lush and aromatic when made from ripe fruit.

```
4 lb ripe mangoes, peeled and sliced
2.5 lb sugar
Juice of 3 lemons
1 tsp pectin enzyme
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Crush mango flesh. Add cold water and pectin enzyme. Proceed as standard fruit wine.

## Flower Wines

Flower wines capture the essence of spring and summer. The technique differs from fruit wines—you're extracting delicate aromatics, not substantial sugars.

### General Flower Wine Method

1. **Harvest at peak bloom**: Early morning after dew dries
2. **Use only petals**: Remove all green parts (bitter)
3. **Steep, don't boil**: Boiling destroys delicate aromatics
4. **Add body**: Flowers provide no sugar, acid, or body—raisins and citrus are essential
5. **Ferment cool**: Preserves aromatics

**Standard Flower Wine Base**
```
1 gallon flower petals, loosely packed
2.5 lb sugar
8-12 oz raisins
Juice of 2-3 lemons or oranges
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Pour warm (not boiling) water over petals. Steep 3-4 days, stirring daily. Strain, pressing gently. Add sugar, citrus, raisins. Ferment as standard.

### Specific Flower Wines

**Elderflower Wine**
The quintessential flower wine—fragrant, delicate, resembling Muscat.

```
1 pint elderflower florets (shake off insects)
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
1 tsp citric acid
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Use only the creamy-white flowers, no stems. Steep in warm water 4 days. Strain, add remaining ingredients. One of the finest country wines; drink within 2 years while fresh.

**Dandelion Wine**
Classic springtime wine, sunny and cheerful.

```
2 quarts dandelion petals (no green)
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 1 orange and 1 lemon
1/2 oz root ginger, bruised
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Pick dandelions at midday when fully open. Remove all green (very bitter). Pour boiling water over petals and ginger. Steep 2 days. Strain, add remaining ingredients. Golden color, mild flavor.

**Rose Petal Wine**
Fragrant and romantic; use heavily scented varieties.

```
2 quarts rose petals (fragrant red or pink varieties)
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Use unsprayed roses, ideally old garden varieties. Remove white heel of each petal. Pour warm water over petals. Steep 5 days. Produces a pink wine with genuine rose perfume.

**Cowslip Wine**
Traditional English wine from Primula veris; now protected in some areas.

```
2 quarts cowslip flowers
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Historical recipe from 1750: "Take a peck of cowslips, pour on them three gallons of water boiling hot; add the juice of three or four lemons, three pounds of loaf sugar, and a small quantity of yeast. Let it work three days, then put it into a cask."

**Clover Wine**
Subtle and honey-like; red clover preferred.

```
2 quarts red clover blossoms
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

**Marigold Wine**
Golden colored with mild, pleasant flavor.

```
2 quarts marigold (calendula) petals
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 oranges
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

## Vegetable Wines

Vegetable wines sound improbable but include some surprisingly good country wines. Root vegetables are particularly successful due to their starch and sugar content.

### Root Vegetable Wines

**Parsnip Wine**
One of the finest vegetable wines, often compared to Madeira or Sherry.

```
4 lb parsnips, scrubbed and sliced
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Boil parsnips until just tender (don't overcook to mush). Strain liquid onto sugar and raisins. Discard parsnips. When cool, add lemon juice and yeast. Ages exceptionally well—2-3 years minimum.

**Carrot Wine**
Golden and slightly sweet, similar to parsnip but lighter.

```
4 lb carrots, scrubbed and sliced
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
1 oz root ginger, bruised
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Proceed as parsnip wine. Ginger complements carrot's sweetness.

**Beetroot Wine**
Dramatic deep red color; earthy, dry wine.

```
4 lb beetroot, scrubbed and sliced
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Boil beetroot until tender. Strain (the liquid will be vivid purple-red). Add sugar while warm. Proceed as standard. The color fades somewhat with age.

**Potato Wine**
Unusual but traditional; clean, neutral flavor.

```
4 lb potatoes, scrubbed and sliced
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
1 oz root ginger
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Boil potatoes until soft. Strain immediately (starch clouds the wine). Add remaining ingredients. Best blended with fruit wines or used as a base.

### Other Vegetable Wines

**Pea Pod Wine**
Made from empty pods after shelling peas—waste not, want not.

```
4 lb pea pods (young, tender pods only)
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Boil pods 30 minutes. Strain onto sugar. A light, fresh wine best drunk young.

**Marrow (Squash) Wine**
Traditional harvest wine.

```
4 lb marrow/squash, peeled and cubed
3 lb demerara sugar
8 oz raisins
1 oz root ginger
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

The traditional method: cut marrow in half, scoop out seeds, fill cavity with demerara sugar, leave overnight. Liquid collects and is fermented. Modern method: boil marrow, strain, add sugar.

## Grain and Other Wines

### Grain Wines

**Wheat Wine**
Full-bodied, sherry-like wine from grain.

```
2 lb wheat berries
2 lb raisins
2.5 lb sugar
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Crush wheat coarsely. Combine with raisins and pour over boiling water. Steep 10 days, stirring daily. Strain, add sugar and lemon. Ferment under airlock. Ages well.

**Barley Wine** (the wine, not the beer style)
Rich and full, distinct from the beer style.

```
1 lb pearl barley
1 lb raisins
2.5 lb sugar
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Boil barley until tender. Strain onto raisins and sugar. Add lemon juice and yeast when cool.

### Raisin Wine

The closest approach to grape wine without fresh grapes.

**Basic Raisin Wine**
```
3 lb raisins (or mixed raisins and sultanas)
2 lb sugar
Juice of 2 lemons
Wine yeast (Lalvin EC-1118)
Water to 1 gallon
```

Chop raisins and pour boiling water over them. Steep 10 days, stirring daily. Strain, pressing well. Add sugar and lemon. A full-bodied, vinous wine.

**Smyrna Raisin Wine** (historical recipe)
```
4 lb Smyrna raisins (or other large, sweet raisins)
1 lb sugar only
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

More raisins, less added sugar, for a more grape-like result.

### Herbal Wines

**Nettle Wine**
Traditional spring tonic wine.

```
2 quarts young nettle tops (use gloves!)
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 lemons
1/2 oz root ginger
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Harvest only young spring nettles (top 4-6 leaves). Boil 30 minutes. Strain onto sugar and raisins. A refreshing, slightly vegetal wine.

**Parsley Wine**
Surprisingly pleasant; use flat-leaf parsley.

```
1 lb fresh parsley, chopped
2.5 lb sugar
8 oz raisins
Juice of 2 oranges and 1 lemon
Wine yeast
Water to 1 gallon
```

Pour boiling water over parsley. Steep 24 hours only (longer becomes bitter). Strain, add remaining ingredients.

## Science and Technology of Fruit Wine Production

### Fermentation Considerations

Country wines differ from grape wine in several biochemical ways:

**Sugar content**: Most fruits contain 5-15% sugar vs. grapes at 20-25%. Sugar addition is essential to reach wine-strength alcohol (10-14% ABV).

**Acid composition**: Grapes contain primarily tartaric acid, which is stable. Other fruits contain:
- Malic acid (apples, stone fruits)—can undergo malolactic fermentation
- Citric acid (citrus, berries)—metabolized by some bacteria
- Various organic acids affecting flavor profile

**Nitrogen content**: Many fruits lack adequate yeast-assimilable nitrogen. Country wines often benefit from nutrient addition, especially:
- Diammonium phosphate (DAP)
- Organic nitrogen sources (Fermaid-O, yeast hulls)

**Pectin**: A major concern in country winemaking. Pectin causes persistent haze and can create "set" (gelled) wine if not treated. Always use pectin enzyme before fermentation for:
- Stone fruits
- Apples and pears
- Berries
- Any fruit with "jammy" texture

### Health Benefits of Fruit Wines

Research in *Science and Technology of Fruit Wine Production* (2017) documents significant bioactive compounds in fruit wines:

**Polyphenolic compounds**: Fruit wines retain and sometimes concentrate the polyphenols from source fruits:
- Anthocyanins (red/purple color compounds) with antioxidant properties
- Flavonoids with cardioprotective effects
- Tannins (especially berry and elderberry wines)

**Antioxidant activity**: Studies show fruit wines can have antioxidant activity comparable to or exceeding grape wines:
- Berry wines (elderberry, blackberry, blueberry) particularly high in total phenolic content (TPC)
- Fermentation can increase bioavailability of some compounds

**Specific health-relevant compounds**:
- Elderberry: Anthocyanins, shown in studies to have antiviral properties
- Cherry: Melatonin content (may aid sleep)
- Blackcurrant: Exceptionally high vitamin C retention

Note: These potential benefits apply to moderate consumption. All alcohol carries health risks, and claims should be evaluated critically.

### Quality Factors in Fruit Wine Production

**Fruit selection**:
- Ripe but not overripe (overripe → off-flavors, wild yeast problems)
- Sound fruit only (mold and rot create serious faults)
- Frozen fruit is acceptable and often preferable (breaks cell walls, kills wild organisms)

**Temperature control**:
- Primary fermentation: 65-75°F (18-24°C) for most fruit wines
- Lower temperatures (55-65°F) preserve delicate aromatics in flower and light fruit wines
- Higher temperatures extract more color from berries but risk volatile loss

**Sulfite use**:
- Unlike grape wine, many country wines are made without sulfites
- For longer aging, 50 ppm SO₂ at bottling improves stability
- See wine-science.md for SO₂ management details

**Aging**:
- Light wines (flower, strawberry): drink within 1 year
- Medium wines (apple, gooseberry): 1-2 years
- Full wines (elderberry, damson, parsnip): 2-5 years
- Some country wines (elderberry, sloe) can age 10+ years

## Troubleshooting Country Wines

| Problem | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---------|--------------|----------|
| Haze that won't clear | Pectin haze | Add pectin enzyme (works slowly once alcohol is present); try two-part finings |
| Fermentation won't start | Low nutrients, high sulfite, wrong temperature | Add yeast nutrient; check temperature; if sulfited, wait 24h before pitching |
| Fermentation stuck | Nutrient depletion, temperature drop, high alcohol | Add nutrient and energizer; warm to 70°F; if very high gravity, may be finished |
| Off-flavors (vinegar) | Acetobacter infection | Prevention: keep vessels full, airlocks filled. If caught early, add sulfite and bottle quickly |
| Oxidized (sherry-like when unwanted) | Air exposure | Keep secondaries full; minimize racking; add sulfite |
| Bitter aftertaste | Green material included, over-extracted | Time helps; blend with neutral wine |
| Set/gelled wine | Pectin; too much fruit without enzyme | Hard to fix after the fact; try adding pectin enzyme and stirring vigorously |

## Historical Country Wine Recipes

### Oxyat (18th Century Honey-Vinegar Drink)
From a 1750s recipe book:
```
"Take three spoonfuls of Honey, and a pint and a half of water; mix it
together with a sprig of Rosemary, and then add two spoonfuls of wine
vinegar; this drink is cooling."
```
A refreshing non-alcoholic (or barely alcoholic) traditional beverage.

### Walnut Mead (Historical)
```
"Take ripe walnuts about the middle of July, bruise them, and put
them into a pot. Pour on them as much boiling water as will cover them,
let them stand until cold, strain them through a hair sieve, sweeten the
liquor with honey, and ferment it."
```

### Cowslip Wine (1750)
```
"Take a peck of cowslips, pour on them three gallons of water boiling
hot; add the juice of three or four lemons, three pounds of loaf sugar,
and a small quantity of yeast. Let it work three days, then put it into
a cask."
```
