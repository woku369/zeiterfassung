# Wild Fermentation Science

*Synthesized from Sandor Ellix Katz's "Wild Fermentation" (2nd ed, 2016) and "The Art of Fermentation" (2012)*

## Philosophy of Wild Fermentation

Wild fermentation relies on organisms spontaneously present on food substrates or in the environment, rather than commercially cultivated, isolated single-species starters. This is the oldest form of fermentation, practiced for millennia before the identification of yeast as the agent of alcohol production.

> "Fermentation processes are ancient rituals that humans have been practicing since long before the dawn of history." — Sandor Katz

### Wild vs. Cultured Fermentation

| Aspect | Wild Fermentation | Cultured Fermentation |
|--------|------------------|----------------------|
| Yeast source | Naturally present on ingredients or airborne | Commercial packets or isolated cultures |
| Predictability | Variable, influenced by environment | Consistent, reproducible |
| Flavor profile | Complex, terroir-driven, often funky/sour | Clean, predictable |
| Speed | Often slower startup | Rapid, vigorous start |
| Historical context | Traditional, pre-modern | Post-industrial |

### Key Principle: Selective Environments

The practice of fermentation consists of manipulating environmental conditions to encourage certain organisms and discourage others:

- **Submersion** – Keeping vegetables/ingredients under liquid excludes oxygen-dependent molds
- **Salt concentration** – Favors lactic acid bacteria, inhibits pathogens
- **Temperature** – Different organisms thrive at different temperatures
- **Acidity** – Developing acid protects from spoilage organisms
- **Air access** – Determines aerobic vs. anaerobic fermentation

## Wild Yeast Biology

### Saccharomyces cerevisiae

The most famous fermentation yeast was the first genome to be completely sequenced. Despite extensive genetic research, its natural history and habitats remain poorly understood.

**Natural habitats:**
- Fruits (especially damaged or overripe)
- Flowers and leaves
- Tree bark and exuded sap
- Insect digestive tracts (especially beetles)
- Oak-tree-associated soils

**Seasonal variation:** Highest populations in summer months. Insects are probably the most important vectors distributing yeasts in nature.

### Kloeckera apiculata

Often dominates early stages of spontaneous fruit fermentation, even in grapes. This "wild" yeast frequently initiates fermentation before S. cerevisiae takes over.

### Alcohol Tolerance Range

- Some wild yeasts tolerate only ~3.5% ABV
- Others can produce up to ~20% ABV
- Most function between these extremes
- Champagne yeasts selected for high tolerance (~15-18%)

## Wild Yeast Sources

### 1. Raw Honey

Raw honey contains abundant yeasts in dormant form. Yeasts remain inactive while water content stays at or below 17%.

> "Above 19% water, honey can be expected to ferment even with only one spore per gram of honey, a level so low as to be very rare." — USDA

**Critical:** Pasteurized or cooked honey kills natural yeasts. For wild mead, honey must be raw and unheated.

### 2. Fruit Skins

Fresh fruits, especially those with edible skins, are covered with fermenting yeasts. The whitish "bloom" visible on grape skins contains abundant yeast populations.

**Best sources:**
- Organic or unsprayed fruit (pesticides can inhibit yeast)
- Berries and small fruits (high surface-area-to-volume ratio)
- Grapes (classic wine yeasts)
- Apples (for cider)

### 3. Ambient Air

Yeasts land on liquid surfaces from the air. To capture airborne yeasts:

- Use a wide-mouth vessel with broad surface area
- Cover with cloth to keep flies out while allowing air circulation
- Stir frequently to incorporate surface yeasts and oxygenate
- Work in fruit-rich environments (near orchards, vineyards)

### 4. Traditional Vessels

Fermentation vessels accumulate yeast populations over time in their porous surfaces:

- **Tarahumara ollas** – Clay pots "learn to boil well" from encrusted organic layers
- **Lacandon ceramic measuring pots** – Passed down for generations, maintaining yeast strains
- **Wooden staves and branches** – Yeasts colonize wood grain; traditionally used to transfer cultures

### 5. Raw Grains

Malted grains, when added raw to a cooked mash, can introduce wild yeasts. This is a common technique in traditional African and Latin American brewing.

## Wild Mead (Honey Wine)

### Simple Wild Mead

The most basic wild fermentation: dilute raw honey with water.

**Basic proportions (by volume):**
- Standard: 1 part honey to 4 parts water
- Light/fruity: 1 part honey to 5-6 parts water
- Strong (Polish półtorak): 1 part honey to 0.5 parts water (age 4+ years)
- Light traditional (Lacandon baälche): 1 part honey to 17 parts water

**Process:**
1. Mix raw honey with room-temperature dechlorinated water
2. Stir or shake vigorously several times daily
3. Cover to keep flies out
4. After 3-5 days, bubbling becomes vigorous
5. Continue stirring until bubbling peaks (7-10 days)
6. Enjoy young, or transfer to air-locked vessel for extended fermentation

### Fruit and Botanical Meads

Fresh fruits serve dual purposes: flavor and yeast source.

**Traditional examples:**
- **T'ej** (Ethiopia) – With gesho (woody hops) twigs
- **Baälche** (Lacandon Maya) – With Lonchocarpus violaceus bark
- **Herbal elixir meads** – Any medicinal or culinary herbs

**Yeast nutrients from botanicals:**
- Acids (citrus, acidic fruits)
- Tannins (raisins, black tea, grape skins)
- Nitrogen compounds
- "Growth factors" that stimulate yeast

## Spontaneous Wine and Cider

### Wild Grape Wine

Freshly pressed raw grape juice ferments spontaneously and quickly. Ripe grapes are so yeast-rich that vigorous fermentation starts almost immediately—often within hours.

**Process at Pratale (Umbria, Italy):**
1. Crush grapes by hand or mechanical roller
2. Leave fermenting juice in open vessels
3. For red wine: leave skins in to darken and add tannins
4. For white wine: strain out skins immediately
5. After initial frothy fermentation, transfer to air-locked vessels

**Young wine traditions:**
- **Sira** (Turkey) – Lightly fermented, still sweet, ~2% ABV
- **Federweisser** (Germany) – Young, partially fermented grape juice

### Wild Cider and Perry

Fresh-pressed raw juice from apples (cider) or pears (perry) ferments spontaneously.

**Key points:**
- Use unpasteurized juice with no preservatives
- Fresh juice is yeast-rich; fermentation starts naturally
- Early days often "boil over" with foam
- Traditional farmhouse cider ferments 6+ months to dryness
- Astringent heirloom varieties reduce tannins through fermentation

**For pasteurized juice:** Add raw fruit pieces with skins as yeast source.

## Wild Yeast Beers

Wild yeast beers represent the original method of beer production, before isolated yeast strains became standard.

### Characteristics

> "At one time, all beers exhibited some level of tart, sour, acidic character. Modern brewing methods helped to virtually eliminate these characteristics in beer." — Jeff Sparrow, *Wild Brews*

Wild-fermented beers tend to be:
- **Sour** – Lactic acid bacteria often participate
- **Complex** – Multiple yeast and bacterial species contribute
- **Variable** – Each batch reflects local microflora
- **Funky** – May have earthy, barnyard, or fruity notes

### Belgian Lambic

The most famous wild yeast beer tradition, surviving in the Senne valley near Brussels.

**Cantillon Brewery process:**
1. Brew only in cool seasons
2. Hot wort cooled in open "coolship" (shallow copper tank)
3. Vented rafters allow airborne yeasts to populate wort
4. Building itself harbors characteristic wild organisms
5. Over 200 different organisms identified in lambic fermentation

### Methods for Introducing Wild Yeast to Beer

Since beer is cooked before fermentation, the grain is not a direct yeast source:

1. **Air exposure** – Open fermentation in yeast-rich environments
2. **Raw malt addition** – Add some uncooked malted grain to cooled wort
3. **Fresh fruit** – Organic fruit with skins
4. **Dedicated vessels** – Never-washed fermentation crocks
5. **Ritual stirring implements** – Carved sticks that harbor yeast in crevices
6. **Previous batch** – Foam or sediment from active fermentation

### Invocation Tradition

> "When the wort is ready, you might leave it out, uncovered, in a container with a wide opening. Then sit near it and begin to talk with the spirit of the yeast—to call on the bryggjemann or kveik to come." — Stephen Harrod Buhner

Traditional brewers often called upon spirits or used ritual to invite fermentation. The carved stirring stick (kveik stick) was hung to dry between batches, harboring yeast in its crevices.

## Indigenous Grain Beverages

### Tesgüino (Tarahumara Malted Corn Beer)

Traditional beer of indigenous Mexican peoples, thick and nutritive.

**Process:**
1. Sprout dry field corn until sprouts reach ~1 inch
2. Coarsely grind sprouted corn into paste
3. Cook paste in water for 8-24 hours (caramelizes sugars)
4. Strain, cool, and ferment

**Yeast introduction:** Traditionally via dedicated olla (clay pot) that is never washed. The vessel's interior becomes "encrusted with a thick organic layer" containing S. cerevisiae.

**Cultural significance:** Tesguinada (tesgüino drinking events) are "the basic social activity" for Tarahumara people, serving as work parties, religious ceremonies, and social gatherings.

### Chicha (Andean Chewed-Corn Beer)

The most ancient method of carbohydrate conversion: human saliva.

**Chewing process:**
1. Cook corn until soft
2. Chew small amounts, saturating with enzyme-rich saliva
3. Form into balls (muko) and spit out
4. Dry the muko for storage
5. Reconstitute and heat to ~155°F (enzyme activation temperature)
6. Boil to kill bacteria, cool, and ferment

> "The chewed variation is said to persist in some Andean communities." — Sandor Katz

**Note:** The hour of boiling after enzyme conversion ensures safety regardless of oral bacteria.

### Sorghum Beer (Africa)

Traditional staple beer across sub-Saharan Africa, more food than beverage.

**Two-stage fermentation:**
1. **Lactic acid fermentation** – Soured mash (12-24 hours)
2. **Alcohol fermentation** – With raw malt introduction

**Process highlights:**
- Malt grains sprouted 2-4 days, then sun-dried
- Porridge made from unmalted grain, enzymes from malt
- Extended cooking caramelizes sugars
- Fresh opaque beer pressurizes quickly—bottle in plastic

**Merissa (Sudanese variant):** Uses dry-roasted sourdough (surij) for distinctive color and smoky flavor. Charring "destroys acid-forming bacteria" to control sourness.

### Asian Rice Brews

Rice fermented using mold enzymes from various cultures:
- **Qu/Jiuqu** (China) – Mixed mold culture balls
- **Koji** (Japan) – Aspergillus oryzae on rice
- **Nuruk** (Korea) – Korean enzyme starter
- **Ragi** (Indonesia/Malaysia)
- **Marcha** (Nepal/India)

**Key distinction:** Saccharification and fermentation occur simultaneously (unlike Western malting where they're sequential).

**Basic process:**
1. Cook rice, cool to body temperature
2. Mix in crushed starter ball
3. Form well in center to collect liquid
4. Ferment in warm spot, stirring daily
5. Liquefaction occurs as enzymes work
6. Strain when desired strength reached

## Practical Wild Fermentation Techniques

### Stirring Protocol

Frequent, vigorous stirring is essential for wild fermentation:

- **Oxygenates** – Yeast growth requires some oxygen
- **Distributes** – Spreads yeast activity throughout
- **Incorporates** – Mixes in airborne yeasts from surface
- **Prevents mold** – Disturbs surface before mold establishes

**Katz's vortex method:** Stir rapidly in one direction to create downward-pulling vortex, then reverse direction.

### Temperature Considerations

| Temperature | Effect |
|-------------|--------|
| Cold (<60°F) | Very slow fermentation, may stall |
| Cool (60-70°F) | Slow, clean fermentation |
| Room temp (70-75°F) | Active, standard fermentation |
| Warm (75-85°F) | Rapid fermentation, more esters |
| Hot (>90°F) | May stress yeast, off-flavors |

Wild fermentation generally works at ambient temperatures. Warmer = faster but less predictable.

### Preventing Vinegar

Acetic acid bacteria (Acetobacter) need oxygen to convert alcohol to vinegar.

**Protection strategies:**
1. Vigorous fermentation releases CO2, protecting surface
2. Air-lock after initial vigorous phase
3. Minimize headspace in vessels
4. Rack to fresh vessels to restart stuck fermentation

If vinegar develops: embrace it as a different product, or use for cooking.

### Troubleshooting Wild Ferments

| Problem | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| No bubbling | Cold temperature | Move warmer, be patient |
| No bubbling | Chlorinated water | Use dechlorinated water |
| No bubbling | Heated/pasteurized substrate | Add raw yeast source |
| Surface mold | Insufficient stirring | Skim mold, stir more frequently |
| Vinegar taste | Air exposure after fermentation slowed | Air-lock earlier; accept as vinegar |
| Stuck fermentation | Yeast stressed or exhausted | Rack to aerate; add nutrients |
| Too sour | Extended fermentation, LAB activity | Ferment shorter; this is often desirable |

## The Continuous Starter Method

Maintain wild fermentation cultures through regular use:

1. Save 1-2 cups of actively fermenting batch
2. Add to next new batch as starter
3. Keep starter refrigerated if taking breaks
4. Feed regularly (like sourdough)
5. Share with others for backup

> "This is how a spontaneous ferment evolves into a starter culture: through persistent engagement." — Sandor Katz

Traditional ferments perpetuated this way represent "long unbroken lineages, coevolved with their human keepers."

## Safety Considerations

### Alcohol Fermentation is Generally Safe

> "Risky is not a word I would use to describe vegetable fermentation. It is one of the oldest and safest technologies we have." — Fred Breidt, USDA microbiologist

The same principle applies to most alcohol ferments from plant sources:
- Yeasts and LAB rapidly dominate sugary liquids
- Acidification destroys potential pathogens
- Alcohol itself is antimicrobial

### Bottle Carbonation Hazards

Actively fermenting beverages sealed in bottles can:
- Overcarbonate explosively
- Cause injury from flying glass
- Create significant mess

**Safety measures:**
- Ferment to dryness before bottling for carbonation
- Add only small priming sugar (1 tsp/quart)
- Use pressure-rated bottles (bail-top, beer bottles, champagne)
- Monitor in plastic bottles by squeezing
- Refrigerate to slow fermentation

### Methanol Concerns

Methanol is produced in small amounts during all fermentation but is only a concern in distillation, where it concentrates. In fermented beverages, methanol levels are naturally low and safe.

## Historical and Esoteric Fermented Beverages

Unusual and forgotten beverages from around the world, many relying on wild or spontaneous fermentation.

*Source: Stewart, Amy. The Drunken Botanist (2013).*

### Root, Tuber, and Starch Beverages

| Beverage | Origin | Substrate | Method |
|----------|--------|-----------|--------|
| **Mobbie** | Barbados (1652) | Sweet potatoes | Fermented with water, lemon juice, sugar. Popular over a century until sweet potato beetles destroyed crops; replaced by rum. |
| **Caowy** | Brazil | Sweet potatoes | Fermented sweet potato drink (Portuguese: *vinho d'batata*). |
| **Parsnip wine** | England | Parsnips | Boiled parsnips + sugar + water, wild yeast fermentation. Stored 6-12 months. Cassell's (1883): "highly spoken of by those accustomed to home-made wines." |
| **Tapai** | Malaysia | Rice cakes | Rice combined with local yeast, fermented in rubber tree leaf wrappers. Mildly alcoholic. |
| **Mudai** | Chile (Pehuenche) | Monkey puzzle tree seeds (*Araucaria araucana*) | Boiled seeds, naturally fermented. Sometimes chewed and spit for salivary amylase conversion. Ceremonial drink poured into special wooden bowls. |

### Sap and Plant Beverages

| Beverage | Origin | Substrate | Method |
|----------|--------|-----------|--------|
| **Ulanzi** | Tanzania | Bamboo shoots | Bashed bamboo shoots ferment naturally in as little as **5 hours**. Only made during rainy spring season. |
| **Birch sap wine** | Northern Europe (since ~800 BC) | Birch sap | John Evelyn (1662): "To every gallon of birch-water put a quart of honey, well stirr'd together; then boil it almost an hour with a few cloves, and a little limon-peel... add three or four spoonfuls of good ale to make it work... bottle it up." |
| **Tej/T'edj** | Ethiopia | Honey | ~6 parts water to 1 part honey, fermented weeks in pottery/gourd vessels. Flavored with bitter buckthorn leaves (*Rhamnus prinoides*). |
| **Date palm wine** | Middle East/Africa | Palm sap | Ferments almost immediately from wild yeast on collection gourds. Reaches balance within hours; bacteria then convert to vinegar. |
| **May wine (Maiwein)** | Germany | White wine + sweet woodruff | Wine infused with sweet woodruff (*Galium odoratum*) in early spring, served at May Day festivals. Harvest woodruff early before coumarin levels rise. |

### Cactus and Fruit Beverages

| Beverage | Origin | Substrate | Method |
|----------|--------|-----------|--------|
| **Colonche** | Mexico (Chichimeca) | Prickly pear cactus juice | Ancient fermented cactus juice. People traveled to follow the bloom cycle. |
| **Navai't** | US Southwest (Tohono O'odham) | Saguaro cactus fruit (*Carnegiea gigantea*) | Fermented wine from saguaro fruit. |
| **Aguardiente de medronho** | Portugal | Strawberry tree fruit (*Arbutus unedo*) | Fermented and distilled. Traditional Portuguese spirit. |

### Historical Cordials and Liqueurs

| Beverage | Era | Ingredients | Notes |
|----------|-----|------------|-------|
| **Rosolio** | 1600 | Sundew (*Drosera rotundifolia*, a carnivorous plant), dates, cinnamon, ginger, cloves, grains of paradise, sugar, rose petals | Sir Hugh Plat's recipe: steep all in "Aqua Composita" in sealed glass vessel for 20 days. Must first "pick out all the black motes from the leaves" (insects). |
| **Capillaire** | 17th c. | Maidenhair fern (*Adiantum capillus-veneris*) syrup | Originally medicinal, evolved into cocktail ingredient. Boil water, pour over fern, stand 30 min, strain, add orange flower water + sugar. **Warning:** Ferns can accumulate arsenic from soil. |
| **Kykeon** | Ancient Greece | Beer, wine, and mead mixed | In Homer's *Odyssey*, Circe used it to drug Odysseus's crew. |
| **Vin Mariani** | 19th c. France | Wine + coca leaves | Endorsed by Sarah Bernhardt and French cardinals. Precursor to modern cola. |
| **Godfrey's Cordial** | 19th c. | Molasses + sassafras oil + laudanum | A popular (and dangerous) cure-all. |
| **Forbidden Fruit** | 20th c. | Pomelo, honey, brandy | Defunct liqueur by Charles Jacquin et Cie. Essential to the classic Tantalus cocktail. |

### Starch Conversion Methods in Traditional Brewing

Different cultures independently developed methods to convert starch to fermentable sugar:

| Method | Region | Mechanism |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| **Malting** | Europe, Middle East | Wetting grain triggers enzymes that convert starch |
| **Koji mold** (*Aspergillus oryzae*) | East Asia | Mold grows into grain, releases amylase enzymes |
| **Salivary amylase** | Americas, Oceania | Chewing and spitting substrate; human saliva enzyme converts starch |
| **Germination + heat** | Africa | Sorghum soaked 1-2 days, spread on leaves to germinate, combined with hot water |

### Earliest Known Fermented Beverages

- **~8000 BC** — Rice, fruit, and honey brew (Jiahu site, Henan Province, China). Recreated with Dogfish Head brewery as Chateau Jiahu.
- **~6000 BC** — Sorghum fermentation in Africa
- **~3000 BC** — Meadowsweet-flavored beer in Europe (pre-hops)
- **~2000 BC** — Date palm wine depicted in Egyptian tomb paintings
- **~800 BC** — Birch sap in European wine vessels

## Summary

Wild fermentation connects us to the oldest human tradition of alcohol production. By understanding the principles—selective environments, yeast biology, and traditional techniques—modern fermenters can produce complex, terroir-driven beverages that reflect their local environment and personal engagement with the microbial world.

> "In reclaiming fermentation, we can take back more than the mere substance of our food and drink. Through fermentation, we can reconnect ourselves to the broader web of life, in spirit and in essence, as well as the physical plane." — Sandor Katz
