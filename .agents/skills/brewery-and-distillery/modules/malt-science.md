## 1. Barley Biology

### Grain Anatomy

A barley kernel is technically a caryopsis (a type of fruit), not a seed. It contains an embryo, a food source (endosperm), and a protective outer shell, analogous to an egg. The kernel consists of distinct layers, each with a role in malting and brewing:

| Structure | % of Kernel Weight | Role in Brewing |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Husk (lemma + palea) | ~10% | Filter bed in lautering; source of polyphenols/tannins |
| Pericarp | <1% (few cells thick) | Protective layer; fused with testa |
| Testa (seed coat) | <1% | Barrier to moisture; concentrates polyphenols |
| Aleurone layer | 2-3 cells thick | Enzyme factory during germination; produces alpha-amylase, glucanase, protease |
| Starchy endosperm | ~75-80% | Energy reserve; source of fermentable extract |
| Embryo | ~4% | Living tissue; initiates germination; produces hormones (gibberellins) |
| Scutellum | <1% | Shield between embryo and endosperm; transfers nutrients |

**The husk** is composed of lignin, pentosans, hemicelluloses, and silica. It is resistant to enzymatic degradation during mashing, which is critical for forming the stable, porous filter bed needed for lautering. Husk material also contains astringent tannins that can contribute harsh flavors, particularly when over-crushed or sparged with hot/alkaline water.

**The aleurone layer** is metabolically active in mature grain. During germination, it responds to gibberellin hormones released by the embryo, producing the hydrolytic enzymes (alpha-amylase, endo-beta-glucanase, proteases, glucoamylase) that break down the endosperm reserves. The aleurone does not act all at once; enzyme production progresses as gibberellins diffuse along the layer from the basal (embryo) end toward the distal end.

**The endosperm** has an internal structure of large and small starch granules embedded in a protein matrix, surrounded by thin cell walls made of beta-glucans (~75%), hemicellulose (~20%), and cellulose (~5%).

### Starch Structure

Starch comprises roughly 60-65% of dry grain weight and exists in two forms:

- **Amylose** (~25-30% of total starch): Unbranched chains of ~2000 glucose units linked by alpha-1,4 bonds. Forms helical structures that are dense and harder to degrade. Binds iodine to produce a blue-black color (the basis of the brewer's iodine starch test).
- **Amylopectin** (~70-75% of total starch): Highly branched molecules with alpha-1,6 linkages roughly every 30 glucose units. Can exceed 100,000 glucose units. More open structure, easier for enzymes to attack.

Starch granules come in two size classes:
- **Large granules**: ~25 micrometers diameter, contain ~90% of starch by weight. Gelatinize at 47-61 deg C (117-142 deg F) in barley.
- **Small granules**: ~5 micrometers diameter, ~90% of granules by number but only ~10% by weight. Gelatinize at 51-92 deg C (124-198 deg F). More readily degraded during malting but less so during mashing.

During malting, enzymes preferentially attack the numerous small granules (high surface area), providing glucose for acrospire growth, while large granules are mostly left intact for the brewer (Mallett, 2014).

### Protein Content and Effects

Barley protein ranges from about 8-16% (dry basis). For malting and brewing:

- **Low protein (8-11%)**: Preferred by European all-malt brewers. Higher starch-to-protein ratio means more extract per pound of grain.
- **Moderate protein (11-13%)**: Typical of quality North American malting barley. Sufficient enzyme and FAN levels for adjunct brewing.
- **High protein (>13%)**: Generally undesirable for brewing. Results in more haze, more nutrients for spoilage organisms, inconsistent results, and less extract per pound.

Higher protein is associated with drought stress, excessive nitrogen fertilizer, and smaller kernel size. Protein content is roughly inverse to extract potential.

### Two-Row vs Six-Row Barley

| Characteristic | Two-Row | Six-Row |
|---------------|---------|---------|
| Kernel arrangement | Single pair of spikelets per node | Three spikelets per node |
| Kernel uniformity | More uniform, plumper | Variable size; lateral kernels smaller |
| Husk percentage | Lower (~10%) | Higher (~13%) |
| Protein | Generally lower | Generally higher |
| Extract potential | Higher per pound | Lower per pound |
| Diastatic power | Moderate (80-160 deg L) | High (120-180+ deg L) |
| Primary use | All-malt brewing, European styles | Adjunct brewing, high-enzyme applications |

Modern breeding has narrowed the gap between two-row and six-row varieties significantly. As Mallett notes, quoting Dan Carey: "a two-row grown on dryland under stressed conditions is not very different from a six-row."

### Winter vs Spring Barley

- **Spring barley**: Planted in spring, harvested in late summer. Most malting barley worldwide is spring-planted.
- **Winter barley**: Planted in fall, overwinters dormant, harvested earlier in summer. Generally 20% higher yields. Requires a cold period (vernalization) before flowering. Winter survivability varies by region and variety.

Winter barley offers benefits including reduced water requirements, soil stabilization, earlier harvest (avoiding late-summer storms), and double-cropping potential. However, winter kill risk and limited approved varieties constrain adoption.

## 2. The Malting Process

Malting consists of three steps -- steeping, germination, and kilning -- that transform raw barley into a brewing resource by initiating controlled germination and then halting it.

### Steeping

**Objective**: Raise barley moisture from ~12% to 43-48%, clean the grain, and initiate metabolism.

The barley is soaked in clean water with alternating periods of immersion and air rest. Air rests provide oxygen to the embryo (which will drown and die without it) and allow CO2 to escape.

**Typical 40-hour steeping schedule** (Mallett):
- 9-hour first immersion
- 9-hour air rest
- 6-hour second immersion
- 6-hour air rest
- 5-hour third immersion
- 5-hour final rest

The grain swells up to 40% by volume during steeping. Water is changed 2-3 times to remove dirt, microbes, and floating debris. Temperature is controlled to manage metabolic rate. Compressed air may be injected to improve oxygen availability and float debris.

Key indicators: Chitting (tiny rootlets emerging from the basal end) may begin during late steeping if aeration has been adequate.

### Germination

**Objective**: Develop enzymes and modify the endosperm structure. Duration: 4-5 days in modern practice (historically up to 2+ weeks).

After steeping, the hydrated barley is spread in germination beds (3-6 inches deep in floor malting; up to 55-60 inches deep in modern pneumatic systems). The grain is periodically turned to prevent rootlet matting and ensure uniform conditions.

**What happens during germination**:
1. The embryo releases gibberellin hormones
2. The aleurone layer responds by producing hydrolytic enzymes
3. Enzymes migrate into the endosperm and begin breaking down cell walls (beta-glucanase), protein matrix (proteases), and starch (alpha-amylase, glucoamylase)
4. Modification progresses from the basal (embryo) end toward the distal end, and from outside in toward center
5. The acrospire grows between the endosperm and husk

**Degree of modification** is assessed by:
- **Acrospire length**: Fully modified malt has acrospire growth of at least 3/4 the kernel length
- **Rubbing out**: Squeezing hydrated kernels -- modified grain is soft and dough-like; unmodified is hard and steely
- **Kolbach Index (S/T ratio)**: Soluble-to-total protein ratio. Values of 38-50% typical for well-modified malt. European brewers may prefer ~38%; adjunct brewers may want 46+.

**Modification is threefold** (Mallett):
1. Dissolution and degradation of endosperm cell walls
2. Degradation of the protein matrix surrounding starch granules
3. Gross hydrolysis of starch granules

### Kilning

**Objective**: Halt germination, remove moisture, develop color and flavor, while preserving key enzymes.

Kilning has two phases:

**Free drying (withering)**: 140-160 deg F (60-71 deg C). Low temperature, high airflow. Removes the majority of moisture. The malt dries from the bottom up. Critical: enzymes are protected because the high moisture keeps the grain cool through evaporative cooling.

**Curing**: After "breakthrough" (when most moisture is gone and exhaust air temperature begins to rise), temperature is increased for color and flavor development.

| Malt Type | Curing Temperature | Final Moisture |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------|
| Pilsner | ~170 deg F (77 deg C) | ~4% |
| Pale / Lager | ~180 deg F (82 deg C) | ~4% |
| Pale Ale | ~185-195 deg F (85-90 deg C) | ~3-3.5% |
| Vienna | ~195-210 deg F (90-99 deg C) | ~3-4% |
| Munich | ~215-230 deg F (102-110 deg C) | ~3-4% |

**Key kilning principles**:
- Raising temperature while the grain is still wet destroys enzymes and produces different malt
- Higher curing temperatures destroy DMS precursors (SMM/DMSO) -- pale ale and Munich malts have low DMS potential
- Higher curing temperatures reduce diastatic power
- Color development is driven by Maillard reactions between amino acids and sugars
- Summer (humid) conditions produce darker malt than winter (dry) conditions at identical kiln settings

**Malting losses**: 100 lbs of barley yields approximately 80 lbs of malt. Losses come from trash/foreign material (~2%), respiration (~6%), rootlet removal (~4%), and moisture reduction (from ~12% to ~4%).

## 3. Malt Analysis and Specifications

The Certificate of Analysis (COA) documents malt quality. Joe Hertrich organizes COA data into five categories (Mallett, 2014):

### Key Analytical Values

| Parameter | Measure | Typical Range (Base Malt) | Significance |
|-----------|---------|--------------------------|--------------|
| **Moisture** | % | 3.5-4.5% | Storage stability; weight economics |
| **Fine Grind Dry Basis (FGDB)** | % extract | 79-83% | Maximum theoretical extract |
| **Coarse Grind As-Is (CGAI)** | % extract | 76-80% | Predicted practical extract |
| **Fine/Coarse Difference** | % | 0.5-1.8% | Indicator of modification (lower = better) |
| **Total Protein** | % | 10-13% | Overall nitrogen content |
| **Soluble Protein** | % | 4.5-6.0% | Protein available in wort |
| **S/T Ratio (Kolbach Index)** | % | 38-50% | Degree of protein modification |
| **FAN** | mg/L or ppm | 180-250 ppm | Yeast nutrient availability |
| **Diastatic Power** | deg Lintner | 80-180 deg L | Starch-converting enzyme power |
| **Alpha Amylase** | DU (20 deg C) | 35-70 DU | Dextrinizing/liquefying power |
| **Color** | SRM/Lovibond | 1.2-6 SRM (varies by type) | Wort color |
| **Beta-Glucan** | ppm | 50-200 ppm | Lautering/filtration indicator |
| **Viscosity** | cP | 1.3-1.6 cP | Flow resistance; modification indicator |
| **Friability** | % | 80-95% | Physical modification; ease of crumbling |

**Color measurement systems**:
- **SRM** (Standard Reference Method): North American standard. Spectrophotometric measurement at 430nm. SRM is based on the Lovibond scale and derived from Congress Mash wort.
- **EBC** (European Brewery Convention): European standard. EBC = (SRM x 1.97). Approximately double SRM values.
- **Lovibond**: Older visual comparison system. Numerically very close to SRM for most practical purposes.

**Diastatic power conversion**: 1 deg Windisch-Kolbach = (deg Lintner x 3.5) - 16

### Interpreting the COA

- **Inadequate protein modification** (low S/T): Slow, difficult lauters; poor extract; bland, turbid wort; erratic fermentation
- **Low carbohydrate modification** (high F/C difference): Steely ends; elevated beta-glucans; milling problems; poor extract
- **Over-modification** (high S/T): Excessive malting losses; reduced foam stability; thin body
- Only ~40 deg L diastatic power is needed to self-convert an all-malt mash (Mallett, citing O'Rourke)

## 4. Base Malts

### Pilsner Malt
- **Color**: 1.2-2 SRM
- **Character**: Very pale, clean, slight green/fresh wort flavor. Retains DMS precursors due to low kilning temperature.
- **Usage**: 100% of grist for Bohemian and German Pilsner, Belgian ales
- **Notes**: Traditional production uses low-protein two-row barley, lower modification, low-temperature/high-airflow kilning

### Pale Malt (Lager Malt)
- **Color**: 1.6-2.8 SRM
- **Character**: Generic light-colored base malt. North American versions optimized for high enzymatic potential and FAN for adjunct brewing. European "pale" or "lager" malts have moderate modification.
- **Usage**: 60-100% of grist
- **Notes**: Very high DP can make fermentability control difficult in all-malt mashes

### Pale Ale Malt
- **Color**: 2.7-3.8 SRM
- **Character**: Well-to-highly modified. Biscuit/toast notes. Low DMS potential from higher kilning. Backbone of English ales.
- **Usage**: 80-100% of grist for English-style ales
- **Notes**: Maritime UK barley varieties respond well to full modification and single-temperature infusion mashing. Exemplar beer: Timothy Taylor's Landlord.

### Vienna Malt
- **Color**: 2.5-4.0 SRM
- **Character**: Rich orange color, slightly toasty and nutty. Refreshing dry finish (unlike crystal malts). Pairs well with noble hops.
- **Usage**: 30-100% of grist for Marzen, Vienna Lager, Festbier
- **Notes**: Sufficient enzymatic power for 100% conversion

### Munich Malt
- **Color**: 3-20 SRM (broad range)
- **Character**: The definitive "malty" flavor. Lighter versions are refined and subtle; darker versions have more heft. Small additions fill out the malt profile of any beer.
- **Usage**: 5-100% of grist; versatile accent malt or full base
- **Notes**: Sufficient enzymatic power for self-conversion even at 100%. Maltol is its characteristic aroma compound.

### Heritage Varieties

**Maris Otter**: English two-row, beloved for rich maltiness and easy lautering. Premium price due to lower yields. Still grown specifically for craft brewing and cask ale. Some brewers believe its superiority is varietal, not merely a result of malting practice.

**Golden Promise**: Scottish semi-dwarf two-row bred from Maythorpe in 1956. Originally for distilling. Recommended variety since 1967. Still grown as premium specialty barley.

## 5. Specialty and Kilned Malts

### Crystal/Caramel Malts

Crystal and caramel malts are produced by "mashing in the husk" -- green (unkilned) malt is heated while still wet, converting internal starches to sugars. The sugars then caramelize and crystallize during roasting.

**Production process** (from Mallett, quoting Briess maltster Dave Kuske):
1. Fully modified green malt loaded at ~40% moisture and 68 deg F (20 deg C)
2. Brought to 158 deg F (70 deg C) over 15 minutes
3. Held 40-45 minutes for conversion (checked by squeezing -- "pop it like a zit")
4. Drying phase: ~2 hours at relatively low temperature as water evaporates
5. Below 15% moisture: color development begins
6. Below 8% moisture: color changes rapidly
7. Ultimate temperature: ~375 deg F (191 deg C) for crystal malts
8. The difference between 10 deg L and 120 deg L is only a matter of minutes in the roaster

**Crystal vs caramel distinction**: "Crystal" properly refers to drum-roasted versions with a glassy, crystallized endosperm and clean candy-like flavors. "Caramel" is a broader class including kiln-made versions, which tend toward more malty/biscuit notes. Kiln-made versions rarely exceed 60 deg L.

| Crystal/Caramel Range | Color (SRM) | Flavor Character | Typical Usage |
|----------------------|-------------|------------------|---------------|
| Extra Light (Carapils) | 1-12 | Body, head retention, slight sweetness | 2-8% |
| Light Crystal | 10-30 | Clean caramel, honey, light toffee | 3-10% |
| Medium Crystal | 30-60 | Pronounced caramel, toffee, some raisin | 3-8% |
| Dark Crystal | 60-90 | Deep caramel, dried fruit, plum | 2-5% |
| Extra Dark Crystal | 90-150+ | Dark fruit, burnt sugar, intense | 1-3% |
| Special B (hybrid) | 50-150 | Raisin, plum, deep dried fruit | 1-5% |

**Practical notes**:
- Lighter crystal malts are more forgiving at higher percentages
- Darker crystal malts can overwhelm a beer in excess; very important to taste before use
- Crystal malts contribute unfermentable dextrins (body and sweetness), not just color
- Color substitution between crystal malts of the same Lovibond from different producers can produce noticeably different flavors

### Melanoidin / Honey / Brumalt
- **Color**: 17-25 SRM
- **Character**: Sweet, honey-like. "Super-Munich." Made by reducing airflow during final germination stages, then kilning at lower temperatures to promote self-conversion.
- **Usage**: Up to 10% for pronounced honey aroma. Low astringency.

### Biscuit / Victory Malt
- **Color**: 20-30 SRM
- **Character**: Bread crust, toasted biscuit, nutty. High in pyrazine compounds. Dry finish.
- **Usage**: 3-10%. Key in nut brown ales. No enzymatic potential.
- **Caution**: Rich in flavor relative to color; easy to overdo

### Amber Malt
- **Color**: 20-36 SRM
- **Character**: Toffee, baked bread, nuts. Some bitterness from pyrazine and pyrrole compounds.
- **Usage**: 5-15%. Traditional English ingredient. Works well with cask mild ales.

### Brown Malt
- **Color**: 40-150 SRM
- **Character**: Similar to amber but more intense. Toffee, baked, nutty. Historical porter ingredient.
- **Usage**: 3-10%. Adds depth and complexity to dark beers. Excessive use causes dry, harsh finish.

## 6. Roasted Malts and Grains

Roasted malts require drum roasters because of the extreme temperatures involved (325-460+ deg F / 160-238+ deg C). The transition from caramel to roasted territory occurs around 325-350 deg F (160-175 deg C).

### Chocolate Malt
- **Color**: 350-500 SRM
- **Character**: Rich coffee and chocolate Maillard products. Mild burnt flavor. Incredible depth with slight astringency.
- **Usage**: 2-8% in porters, brown ales, stouts
- **Notes**: Made by roasting dry finished base malt

### Black Malt (Black Patent)
- **Color**: 435-550 SRM
- **Character**: Acrid, bitter, dry, burnt. Defines the stout flavor profile. Paradoxically, over-roasting destroys both flavor and color as compounds turn to charcoal.
- **Usage**: 2-5% in stouts. Small amounts for color adjustment.
- **From Kuske** (Mallett): "If you are not careful, black malt can go from 600 deg L to 150 deg L in about a minute" from over-roasting.

### Roasted Barley
- **Color**: 300-650 SRM
- **Character**: Not technically a malt (raw barley is roasted). Generally milder than roasted malts. Cocoa-mocha flavors. Produces lighter-colored head foam than equivalent colored malts.
- **Usage**: 5-10% as the defining ingredient in Dry Irish Stout. Also used for color in red ales.

### Dehusked / Carafa Malts
- **Color**: 300-550 SRM (Carafa I, II, III)
- **Character**: Dark color with minimal harshness. The husk material (which contains astringent tannins) is removed before roasting. "No one will ever know it is in your beer" (Mallett).
- **Usage**: 1-5%. Adds black/red color without roasted flavor. Excellent for black lagers, schwarz, and any beer needing color without bitterness.

### Color vs Flavor Extraction

Color extracts rapidly from roasted grains with minimal contact time. Flavor (especially harsh, astringent compounds) extracts more slowly. Techniques to maximize color while minimizing harshness:
- Cold-steeping roasted grains separately and adding the liquid at packaging
- Adding roasted grains only at the end of the mash or in the vorlauf
- Using dehusked versions (Carafa, Midnight Wheat)

## 7. Alternative Grains

### Gelatinization Temperatures

Starch must gelatinize (granules swell, rupture, and disperse) before enzymes can efficiently convert it. Gelatinization temperatures determine whether a grain can be directly mashed with barley malt or requires pre-cooking.

| Grain | Gelatinization Range | Pre-Cooking Required? |
|-------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Barley (malted) | 140-153 deg F (60-67 deg C) | No |
| Wheat | 126-147 deg F (52-64 deg C) | No |
| Rye | 135-158 deg F (57-70 deg C) | No |
| Oats | 127-138 deg F (53-59 deg C) | No (if flaked/rolled) |
| Corn/Maize | 144-165 deg F (62-74 deg C) | Yes (grits); No (flaked) |
| Rice | 142-172 deg F (61-78 deg C) | Yes (grits); No (flaked) |
| Sorghum | 156-167 deg F (69-75 deg C) | Yes |
| Millet | 129-176 deg F (54-80 deg C) | Varies by type |
| Potato starch | 133-156 deg F (56-69 deg C) | No |

Source: Briggs (1998), compiled from multiple references.

### Wheat (Malted and Raw)
- **Protein**: 8-18% (higher than barley on average)
- **Husk**: None (free-threshing). Contributes to lautering problems.
- **Character**: Enhances foam formation and retention (high protein). Slightly bready flavor. Contributes haze.
- **Malted wheat**: Used in Hefeweizen (50%+), Witbier, Berliner Weisse, American Wheat
- **Unmalted (raw) wheat**: Traditional in Belgian Witbier (up to 50%). Requires mashing enzymes from malt.
- **Types**: Hard/soft, red/white, winter/summer. Red wheat has more phenols and tannins. Soft white wheat is generally preferred for brewing.
- **Practical notes**: Rice hulls recommended at >30% wheat to prevent stuck lauters. Ferulic acid concentrated in wheat is the precursor to 4-vinyl guaiacol (clove aroma in Hefeweizen).

### Rye (Malted and Raw)
- **Protein**: 9-15%
- **Character**: Distinctive spicy flavor. Pairs well with bold American hops.
- **Challenges**: Lacks a husk. Extremely gummy when hydrated -- releases viscous starches. Compacts in steep tanks. Germination time varies 2-4 days on the floor.
- **Usage**: 5-20% for rye character. Rice hulls strongly recommended.
- **Malted rye**: Available in colors up to 250 SRM

### Oats (Flaked, Malted, or Steel-Cut)
- **Fat content**: ~6% (highest of common brewing grains) -- can cause staling
- **Beta-glucan**: 3-6% (very high)
- **Character**: Full, soft, silky mouthfeel. Malted versions (e.g., Simpsons Golden Naked Oats) add granola-like flavor.
- **Usage**: 5-15% for mouthfeel. Key ingredient in oatmeal stout. Higher amounts complicate lautering.
- **Storage**: Turn over inventory quickly due to high oil content

### Corn/Maize
- **Starch**: 70-74% (high)
- **Usage**: Flaked maize adds directly to mash. Corn grits require cereal cooking (boiling with 5-10% malt or bacterial alpha-amylase before adding to main mash).
- **Character**: Lightens body and color. Slightly sweet. Traditional in American lager, cream ale.
- **Forms**: Flaked (pre-gelatinized, add directly to mash), grits (require cereal cooker)

### Rice
- **Starch**: 57-81% depending on processing
- **Usage**: Similar to corn -- lightens body and flavor. Rice grits require cereal cooking due to high gelatinization temperature.
- **Character**: Very neutral. Produces a crisp, dry beer.
- **Notes**: Long-grain rice gelatinizes higher (160-165 deg F / 71-74 deg C) than short-grain (149-154 deg F / 65-68 deg C)

### Sorghum and Millet
- **Usage**: Important in gluten-free brewing and traditional African beers
- **Challenges**: Sorghum malts have low amylase levels compared to barley. Sorghum starch has high gelatinization temperature (131-167 deg F / 55-75 deg C). May contain proanthocyanidin tannins that reduce enzyme activity.
- **Millet**: Gelatinization ranges vary by species. Finger millet: 145-165 deg F (63-74 deg C); pearl millet: 134-162 deg F (57-72 deg C).

### Spelt and Triticale
- **Spelt**: Ancient wheat relative (hexaploid). Can be malted. Higher protein and fiber than modern wheat.
- **Triticale**: Wheat-rye hybrid. Can be malted but produces worts with high pentosan content causing high viscosity (Briggs, 1998). Experimental usage.

## 8. Adjunct Sugars

Sugars bypass the mash entirely and are dissolved directly in the kettle (copper adjuncts) or at fermentation. They increase alcohol without adding body.

| Sugar/Extract | Fermentability | Color Contribution | Flavor Effect |
|--------------|---------------|-------------------|---------------|
| **DME (Dry Malt Extract)** | ~70-75% | Varies (2-10+ SRM) | Malty; mirrors the base malt used |
| **LME (Liquid Malt Extract)** | ~70-75% | Varies | Similar to DME; ~20% water |
| **Dextrose (Corn Sugar)** | ~100% | 0 SRM | Neutral; dries out beer |
| **Sucrose (Table Sugar)** | ~100% | 0 SRM | Neutral; dries out beer |
| **Invert Sugar** | ~100% | 0-50+ SRM | Light caramel (dark versions); traditional English |
| **Belgian Candi Sugar (light)** | ~100% | 0-5 SRM | Neutral; raises ABV without body |
| **Belgian Candi Syrup (dark)** | ~90-95% | 40-180+ SRM | Dark fruit, caramel, toffee, fig |
| **Honey** | ~95% | 2-8 SRM | Floral, delicate (mostly ferments dry) |
| **Molasses** | ~60-70% | 80+ SRM | Heavy, bittersweet, mineral |
| **Maple Syrup** | ~90-95% | 35+ SRM | Subtle woody sweetness; ferments mostly dry |
| **Lactose** | ~0% (unfermentable) | 0 SRM | Residual sweetness, body |

**Key principles**:
- Highly fermentable sugars (dextrose, sucrose) thin body and increase ABV. Classic technique in Belgian strong ales (up to 20% of fermentables) and English bitters (invert sugar).
- Invert sugar is sucrose hydrolyzed to its component glucose and fructose. Named for the inversion of optical rotation -- sucrose rotates polarized light clockwise; the mixture rotates it counter-clockwise because fructose is more strongly laevorotatory (Mallett).
- Honey flavor is largely lost during fermentation. Use at flameout or secondary for aroma.
- Lactose is unfermentable by Saccharomyces and contributes residual sweetness and body (milk stout, pastry stout).

## 9. Enzymatic Activity in the Mash

All enzymes are proteins with highly specific three-dimensional structures. Heat causes denaturation (unfolding), destroying function. Each enzyme has optimal temperature and pH ranges where it works fastest before being destroyed.

### Key Mash Enzymes

| Enzyme | Optimal Temp | Optimal pH | Destroyed Above | Function |
|--------|-------------|-----------|-----------------|----------|
| **Phytase** | 86-126 deg F (30-52 deg C) | 5.0-5.5 | 131 deg F (55 deg C) | Lowers mash pH by releasing phosphoric acid |
| **Beta-glucanase** | 95-113 deg F (35-45 deg C) | 4.5-5.0 | 122 deg F (50 deg C) | Degrades beta-glucan cell walls |
| **Proteases** | 113-131 deg F (45-55 deg C) | 4.6-5.3 | 150+ deg F (65+ deg C) | Break down proteins into peptides and amino acids |
| **Ferulic acid esterase** | 113 deg F (45 deg C) | 5.5 | ~140 deg F (60 deg C) | Releases ferulic acid precursor for 4-VG (clove) |
| **Beta-amylase** | 131-150 deg F (55-65 deg C) | 5.0-5.5 | 158 deg F (70 deg C) | Nibbles maltose from non-reducing chain ends |
| **Alpha-amylase** | 149-158 deg F (65-70 deg C) | 5.3-5.7 | 167 deg F (75 deg C) | Chops starch chains at random alpha-1,4 bonds |
| **Limit dextrinase** | 140-149 deg F (60-65 deg C) | 5.1 | 149 deg F (65 deg C) | Cleaves alpha-1,6 branch points (debranching) |

### How the Amylases Interact

**Alpha-amylase** is an endo-enzyme: it "chops" starch chains at random internal alpha-1,4 linkages (but cannot act within one glucose unit of an alpha-1,6 branch point). Acting alone, it produces a wide range of chain lengths with less than 20% fermentable sugar (Mallett).

**Beta-amylase** is an exo-enzyme: it "nibbles" maltose units (two glucose molecules) from the non-reducing end of chains. It cannot cross alpha-1,6 branch points or act within 3 glucose units of one. Acting alone with alpha-amylase, fermentability reaches ~70%.

**Limit dextrinase** (debranching enzyme) cleaves alpha-1,6 branch points, creating new non-reducing ends for beta-amylase to attack. Adding debranching activity raises fermentability to ~80%.

**Glucoamylase** (alpha-glucosidase) removes individual glucose units from non-reducing ends. Concentrated in the embryo and aleurone. Unstable above 104 deg F (40 deg C) in solution but reportedly survives kilning. Plays a minor role in mashing.

### Practical Mash Temperature Effects

| Mash Temp | Result | Mechanism |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| 145-150 deg F (63-65 deg C) | Highly fermentable, dry beer | Beta-amylase favored; more maltose production |
| 150-154 deg F (65-68 deg C) | Balanced fermentability | Both amylases active; moderate dextrin/maltose ratio |
| 154-158 deg F (68-70 deg C) | Less fermentable, fuller body | Beta-amylase rapidly destroyed; alpha-amylase produces dextrins |
| 158-162 deg F (70-72 deg C) | Very full-bodied, sweet | Minimal beta-amylase; high dextrin content |

**Important factors**:
- Beta-amylase is largely destroyed within 40-60 minutes at 149 deg F (65 deg C) in mash conditions (Briggs, 1998)
- Thicker mashes protect enzymes via substrate concentration (more grain = more substrate = more protection from denaturation)
- Calcium ions are essential for alpha-amylase function (~35 mg/L typically present in 1.040 wort from malt alone)
- pH below 5.2-5.4 favors amylase activity; typical mash pH target is 5.2-5.6
- Most chemical reaction rates double with each 18 deg F (10 deg C) increase in temperature, but so does the rate of enzyme destruction

### Typical Wort Composition (Mallett)

From an all-malt mash: 41% maltose, 14% maltotriose, 6% maltotetraose, 6% sucrose, 9% glucose and fructose, 22% dextrins (unfermentable), 2% non-starchy polysaccharides.

## 10. Practical Grain Bill Design

### General Principles

- **Base malt**: Should constitute at least 60-80% of the grist (often higher). Provides the bulk of fermentable extract and necessary enzymes.
- **Specialty kilned malts** (Vienna, Munich, etc.): Can be used up to 100% if they have sufficient enzymatic power. Typically 5-30%.
- **Crystal/caramel malts**: Generally 3-15% of grist. Light versions more forgiving at higher rates; dark versions (>60L) should be used sparingly.
- **Roasted malts**: Rarely exceed 10% of the grist. Often 2-5% is sufficient for significant color and flavor impact.
- **Adjunct grains**: Depends on style -- up to 50%+ with sufficient enzymatic base malt, but watch for lautering issues.

### Style-Based Grain Bill Frameworks

| American Pale Ale | 85-95% 2-Row | 0-10% Munich/Vienna | 3-8% (10-40L) | 0% | 0-5% |
| English Bitter | 85-95% Maris Otter/PA | 0-5% | 3-8% (30-60L) | 0% | 0-5% invert sugar |
| German Pilsner | 100% Pilsner | 0% | 0% | 0% | 0% |
| Marzen/Oktoberfest | 40-60% Vienna/Munich | 40-60% (combined) | 0-5% | 0% | 0% |

### Extract Efficiency

- **Brewhouse efficiency** varies from 60-85% depending on equipment and technique
- **FGDB** (Fine Grind Dry Basis) represents theoretical maximum extract
- **CGAI** (Coarse Grind As-Is) is a more practical predictor
- For recipe design, assume 70-75% efficiency (homebrewing) or 78-85% (commercial) when calculating grain quantities
- Points per pound per gallon (PPG): Base malt = ~36-38 PPG at 100% efficiency. Multiply by your efficiency for actual yield.
- Crystal malts yield somewhat less extract than base malts (~33-34 PPG)
- Roasted malts yield less still (~25-30 PPG) -- much of the organic material has been transformed by roasting

### Mash Thickness Effects

- **Thicker mash** (1.0-1.25 qt/lb): Enzymes are more concentrated and protected from denaturation by substrate. Tends to produce less fermentable wort. Better enzyme survival.
- **Thinner mash** (1.5-2.0+ qt/lb): Enzymes are more dilute and less protected. Tends to produce more fermentable wort. Faster extraction but faster enzyme destruction.
- **Typical range**: 1.25-1.5 qt/lb (2.6-3.1 L/kg) for most brewing

### Color Calculations

The Morey equation approximates beer color from grain bill:

SRM = 1.4922 x (MCU)^0.6859

Where MCU (Malt Color Units) = sum of (weight of grain in lbs x color in Lovibond) / volume in gallons

This equation is reasonably accurate from 1-50 SRM. Above 50 SRM, color perception becomes less meaningful and all beers appear opaque black.

## 11. Grain Handling and Storage

### Crush Size and Milling

The goal of milling is to expose the starchy endosperm while keeping husks as intact as possible:
- **Husk**: Should be cracked open but remain in large pieces for the filter bed
- **Endosperm**: Should be reduced to small, uniform particles for efficient extraction
- These goals inherently conflict; the brewer must balance extract yield against lautering performance

**Mill gap settings** (2-roller mill): Typically 0.035-0.045 inches (0.9-1.1 mm) for homebrewing. Finer gaps increase extract but risk stuck sparges. BIAB (brew in a bag) can use finer crushes.

**Grist composition targets** (6-roller mill, traditional lautering):
- Husks: 20-30%
- Coarse grits: 10-20%
- Fine grits: 30-40%
- Flour: 10-20%

### Malt Conditioning

Malt conditioning (briefly wetting husks before milling) can improve husk integrity during crushing. Spray a fine mist of water (~2% by weight) onto the grain 10-30 minutes before milling. This makes husks pliable rather than brittle, reducing shatter. Particularly useful for high-modification malts with fragile husks.

### Storage Conditions

- **Moisture**: Finished malt is ~4% moisture and quite stable. Keep dry. Any wetting triggers enzymatic activity and mold growth.
- **Temperature**: Store cool if possible, but room temperature is acceptable for normal turnover periods
- **Shelf life**: Properly stored whole-kernel malt keeps well for 6-12 months. Crushed malt begins losing freshness within days to weeks -- mill as close to brew day as possible.
- **Pest control**: Store bags off the floor and away from walls. Seal bins properly. Clean spills immediately. Birds, rodents, and insects (Indian Meal moths, Confused Flour beetles) are attracted to malt.
- **Oats and corn**: Higher oil content means shorter shelf life; turn over inventory more quickly than barley malts.
- **Crystal malts**: Some brewers believe these have shorter optimal shelf life than base malts; store in smaller quantities.

### Detecting Stale or Damaged Malt

- **Visual**: Mold growth, discoloration, broken kernels, excessive dust, presence of insects or webbing
- **Aroma**: Fresh malt should smell clean and pleasant (bready, biscuity, sweet). Off aromas include cardboard, damp paper, musty, sour, rancid, or solvent-like notes.
- **Chewing test**: Always chew malt before brewing. Fresh base malt should taste lightly sweet and starchy with clean cereal character. Stale malt tastes cardboard-like or harsh.
- **Physical**: Poorly stored malt may clump from moisture absorption. If kernels are soft or rubbery rather than crisp, moisture has been absorbed.
- **Freshly kilned malt**: Even fresh malt benefits from 3+ weeks of aging after kilning to allow moisture equalization. Freshly kilned malt often performs poorly in the brewhouse (Mallett).

## 12. Smoked Malts and Smoked Beer

### Smoke Chemistry

Smoke flavor in malt comes from phenolic compounds produced during pyrolysis (thermal decomposition) of wood lignin. The primary smoke-flavor compounds are:

| Compound | Flavor Character | Notes |
|----------|-----------------|-------|
| **Guaiacol** | Smoky, earthy, medicinal | High impact at low ppm; primary smoke marker |
| **4-Methylguaiacol** | Woody, vanilla, ashy | Key smoke compound alongside guaiacol |
| **Syringol** | Ashy, smoky | More abundant from hardwood (angiosperm) smoke |
| **Phenol** | Medicinal, band-aid | Highest concentration but lower sensory activity |
| **Cresols** (o-, m-, p-) | Tar, asphalt, burnt | Higher in peat-smoked malts |
| **4-Ethylguaiacol** | Smoky, creosote | Minor contributor |

Wood type determines the smoke profile: **Softwoods** (gymnosperms) produce more guaiacol from their lignin. **Hardwoods** (angiosperms) produce more syringol. Peat produces high levels of phenol and cresols alongside guaiacols, giving a more intense, medicinal character.

### Commercial Smoked Malts

| Malt | Wood Source | Color (°L) | Smoke Intensity | Character | Recommended Usage |
|------|-----------|-----------|----------------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Weyermann Beechwood Rauchmalz** | Beechwood | 3-6 | Moderate | Sweet, bacon, campfire | 20-100% (the classic Bamberg malt) |
| **Weyermann Oak Smoked Wheat** | Oak | 3-5 | Moderate-light | Smooth, rounded, subtle | 5-50% |
| **Briess Cherry Wood Smoked** | Cherry | 5 | Moderate | Fruity, sweet smoke | 10-50% |
| **Briess Mesquite Smoked** | Mesquite | 5 | Strong | Assertive, BBQ-like | 5-30% (use judiciously) |
| **Peat-smoked malt** (whisky malt) | Peat | 3-5 | Very strong | Medicinal, phenolic, iodine | 1-5% maximum (extremely intense) |

**Smoked malt is a base malt** — it has full diastatic power and can convert itself in the mash. It can be used at any percentage from 1% to 100%.

**Freshness matters**: Smoke character fades over time. Use fresh smoked malt for maximum impact. Older smoked malt produces noticeably less smoke flavor.

### DIY Smoking Malt at Home

Home-smoked malt is typically more intense than commercial smoked malt.

**Method** (Weber grill, cold smoker, or dedicated smoker):
1. Spread base malt (pale or Pilsner) in a single layer on a fine-mesh screen or perforated pan
2. Use soaked wood chips or chunks — **no lighter fluid or self-lighting charcoal**
3. Keep temperature **below 180°F (82°C)** to preserve diastatic enzymes
4. Smoke for 1-3 hours, stirring occasionally for even exposure
5. Dry the malt thoroughly before storage (smoke adds moisture)

**Wood selection**:

| Wood | Smoke Character | Pairs Well With |
|------|----------------|----------------|
| Beechwood | Classic Bamberg, balanced | Märzen, lager, porter |
| Cherry | Sweet, fruity, mild | Porter, brown ale, wheat |
| Apple | Light, sweet, delicate | Wheat beer, session ales |
| Oak | Round, smooth, vanilla | Stout, porter, scotch ale |
| Hickory | Assertive, bacon | Porter, strong ale |
| Alder | Delicate, woody | Lighter beers, seafood pairing |
| Mesquite | Strong, pungent | Use sparingly; smoked porter |
| Pecan | Sweet, nutty, mild | Brown ale, amber |
| Maple | Sweet, mild | Amber, wheat |

**Tips**: Use chlorine-free water to soak wood chips. Use fine mesh to protect malt from ash and tar. Cool fire with no glowing coals or flames — cold smoke is best for preserving enzymes.

### Smoked Beer Styles

| Style | Smoked Malt % | OG | ABV | IBU | Character |
|-------|--------------|-----|-----|-----|-----------|
| **Bamberg Rauchbier** (Märzen) | 50-100% beechwood | 1.050-1.057 | 4.8-6.0 | 20-30 | Dominant smoke over clean Märzen base |
| **Grätzer/Grodziskie** | 100% oak-smoked wheat | 1.028-1.036 | 2.5-3.6 | 20-35 | Light, crisp, effervescent, Polish |
| **Lichtenhainer** | ~50% smoked wheat | 1.032-1.040 | 3.5-4.7 | 5-12 | Sour smoked wheat; lactic tartness |
| **Smoked Porter** | 15-50% | 1.050-1.065 | 5.0-6.5 | 20-40 | Smoke complements dark malt |
| **Smoked Helles** | 20-40% | 1.044-1.052 | 4.5-5.5 | 16-22 | Subtle smoke, clean lager base |
| **Smoked Bock** | 30-60% | 1.064-1.072 | 6.0-7.5 | 20-30 | Rich, malty, substantial smoke |

**Schlenkerla** (Bamberg) brews with 100% beechwood-smoked malt they kiln themselves. **Spezial** (also Bamberg) uses a lighter hand, producing more subtle smoke character.

### Brewing with Smoked Malt

**Grain bill percentages** (beechwood Rauchmalz):
- **1-5%**: Smoky highlights that accentuate other flavors without dominating
- **~11%**: Mild smoke presence (Carton Brewing's approach for some styles)
- **20-30%**: Noticeable smoke that shares the stage with other malt character
- **~45%**: Strong smoke — a good target for a first Rauchbier (Brülosophy finding)
- **60-80%**: Heavy smoke; experienced Rauchbier brewers' range
- **100%**: Traditional Bamberg Rauchbier — full smoke expression

**Peat-smoked malt** is vastly more intense — keep below **5%**, often **1%** is sufficient. Using peat malt at Rauchmalz percentages is a common beginner mistake.

**Smoke perception is non-linear**: Above ~50-60%, increasing smoked malt percentage produces diminishing returns in perceived smokiness.

**Water chemistry**: Smoked beers are malt-forward. Favor chloride over sulfate:
- Sulfate: 50-75 ppm (keep low to avoid harsh/sulfury clash with smoke)
- Chloride: 75-100 ppm (supports malt character)
- Sulfate:chloride ratio: 0.5-0.7

**Hopping**: Restrained bitterness with noble hops. Some brewers add slightly more hops than typical for the base style, finding that moderate bitterness accentuates and balances smoke character. Harsh, burnt, charred, rubbery, sulfury, or phenolic smoke is a fault (BJCP).

**Yeast**: Clean, neutral strains that don't compete with smoke character. German lager yeast (34/70) for Rauchbier. Avoid highly estery or phenolic strains.

**Fermentation**: Cool and clean. Higher temperatures produce esters that can clash with smoke character.

**Aging**: Smoke character evolves over time — typically intensifies in the first few weeks, then gradually fades over months. Brew to style within 1-3 months for optimal smoke expression.

### Common Mistakes

1. Using peat-smoked malt thinking it's beechwood — completely different intensity and character
2. Too much smoked malt for a first attempt — start at 20-30% and adjust upward
3. Not accounting for smoke fade over time — freshness of malt and beer both matter
4. Over-hopping — aggressive bitterness clashes with smoke
5. Using overly estery yeast strains
6. Ignoring water chemistry — high sulfate accentuates harsh smoke edges

## Quick Reference: Maillard Reactions in Malting

The Maillard reaction is the primary driver of color and flavor development in kilned and roasted malts. It occurs between amino acids (from protein breakdown) and reducing sugars at elevated temperatures:

1. **Step 1**: Amino acid + sugar combine (with loss of water) to form an unstable compound
2. **Step 2**: Amadori rearrangement produces a ketosamine intermediate
3. **Step 3**: Three possible pathways:
   - **Pathway A**: Further dehydration produces caramelization-like compounds
   - **Pathway B**: Loss of three water molecules + reaction with amino acids produces melanoidins (large, colored polymeric compounds)
   - **Pathway C**: Strecker degradation produces heterocyclic compounds including pyrazines (nutty), furans (caramel), maltol (characteristic Munich malt aroma), and pyrroles (bread crust)

True caramelization (pyrolysis of sugars without amino acids) requires temperatures above 250 deg F (120 deg C) and low moisture -- conditions rarely achieved in normal malting. Most "caramel" flavors in malt are actually Maillard products.

Nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds, including nitrosamines (carcinogens), become more abundant above 350 deg F (180 deg C). Modern malthouses use indirect kilning (heat exchangers) to prevent combustion gases from contacting the malt, avoiding nitrosamine formation.
