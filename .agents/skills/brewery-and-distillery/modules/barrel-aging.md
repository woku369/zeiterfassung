# Barrel Aging Science and Protocols

Barrel aging transforms wine, spirits, and beer through controlled oxidation and extraction of wood compounds. This module covers the science of barrel maturation, practical management protocols, and rotation schedules.

## The Science of Barrel Aging

### What Happens During Barrel Aging

Seven key processes occur simultaneously in a barrel:

1. **Oxygen ingress**: Air permeates through wood pores and the bunghole
2. **Compound extraction**: Lignin degradation products, tannins, and volatile compounds dissolve into the liquid
3. **Evaporation**: Water and alcohol escape through the wood ("angel's share")
4. **Polymerization**: Phenolic compounds combine into larger, softer molecules
5. **Oxidation reactions**: Aldehydes form, color compounds transform
6. **Esterification**: New flavor compounds develop over time
7. **Microbial activity**: Brett, acetic acid bacteria, or MLF organisms may act

### Oxygen Transfer Rates

From Nevares and Del Alamo's research, oxygen diffusion in **new French oak barrels** ranges from **1.66-2.5 mL/L/month**. This decreases with barrel age as pores become clogged with tartrates and phenolic compounds.

| Container Type | Dissolved O₂ | Mean EH (mV) |
|----------------|--------------|--------------|
| New barrel (1 year) | 0.4 mg/L | 245 |
| Barrel (2 years) | 0.2 mg/L | 228 |
| Barrel (3 years) | 0.2 mg/L | 218 |
| Stainless steel vat | <0.1 mg/L | 220 |
| Concrete vat | <0.1 mg/L | 215 |

*Source: Vivas and Glories, 1993; Handbook of Enology*

### Temperature Effects

Temperature profoundly affects aging reactions:

**At 12°C (54°F)**:
- Color intensity increases (OD 620% rises)
- Blue/purple hues preserved
- Tannin polymerization is slow
- Anthocyanin-tannin complexes form stably
- Recommended for preserving freshness

**At 25°C (77°F)**:
- Color shifts toward orange/yellow
- Rapid tannin polymerization
- Increased risk of anthocyanin breakdown
- Higher microbial risk (volatile acidity)
- Dangerous for barrel aging

**Optimal range**: 12-18°C (54-64°F) with stable humidity around 70-80%.

## Wood Chemistry

### Key Extractable Compounds

Compounds extracted from oak contribute distinctive flavors:

| Compound | Sensory Descriptor | Origin |
|----------|-------------------|--------|
| **Vanillin** | Vanilla | Lignin degradation (toasting) |
| **Eugenol** | Clove, spice | Lignin degradation |
| **Guaiacol** | Smoke, medicinal | Lignin degradation (charring) |
| **Syringaldehyde** | Woody, sour | Lignin degradation |
| **Furfural** | Caramel, almond | Hemicellulose degradation |
| **Oak lactones** | Coconut, woody | Native to oak heartwood |
| **Ellagitannins** | Astringency, structure | Hydrolyzable tannins |

### Toasting vs. Charring

**Toasting** (European method, wine barrels):
- 45 minutes slow heat over oak fire
- Degrades lignin to release vanillin, syringaldehyde
- Moderate penetration (2-3mm)
- Produces caramel, toast, vanilla notes

**Charring** (American method, bourbon barrels):
- High-heat gas flame, typically 15-45 seconds
- Creates activated charcoal layer
- Deeper thermal gradient (up to 6mm)
- Higher volatile phenols (guaiacol, syringol)
- Char layer filters harsh congeners

Char levels for spirits:
- Level 1: Light char (15 seconds)
- Level 2: Medium char (30 seconds)
- Level 3: Medium-plus char (35 seconds) — most common for bourbon
- Level 4: Heavy char (55 seconds) — "alligator char"

### Barrel Seasoning

Before coopering, staves are seasoned outdoors for **1-3 years**. During this period:
- Rain washes away harsh, astringent tannins
- Sun causes structural degradation
- Fungi (white rot) release free phenolics from lignin
- Moisture content decreases
- Overall extractable tannin content drops while desirable aromatics increase

French oak is typically seasoned longer (24-36 months) than American oak (6-24 months).

## Standard Barrel Sizes and Surface Area

Surface area to volume ratio determines extraction rate.

| Barrel Type | Volume | SA/V Ratio | Primary Use |
|-------------|--------|------------|-------------|
| Barrique (Bordeaux) | 225L (59 gal) | ~90 cm²/L | Wine |
| Burgundy pièce | 228L (60 gal) | ~88 cm²/L | Wine |
| Hogshead | 300L (79 gal) | ~75 cm²/L | Wine, spirits |
| Puncheon | 500L (132 gal) | ~60 cm²/L | Sherry, rum |
| Standard bourbon | 200L (53 gal) | ~90 cm²/L | Bourbon |
| Quarter cask | 50L (13 gal) | ~140 cm²/L | Accelerated aging |

### Small Barrel Considerations

Research by Jeffery (2012) on 2, 3, 5, and 10-gallon barrels confirmed:

- **Extraction rate is proportional to SA/V ratio**
- Smaller barrels extract oak compounds faster
- Volume loss is also proportional to SA/V (greater in smaller barrels)
- Small barrels do NOT produce identical results to longer aging in standard barrels
- They extract quickly but lack the slow oxidation reactions of traditional aging

**Volume loss rates**:
- Standard 53-gallon barrel: ~5% per year
- 10-gallon barrel: ~8-10% per year
- 5-gallon barrel: ~12-15% per year
- 2-gallon barrel: ~20%+ per year

For craft distillers using small barrels, plan for higher losses and faster extraction. Small barrels are best for **spirit finishing** (3-6 months) rather than primary maturation.

## Barrel Management Protocols

### New Barrel Preparation

**For wine barrels**:
1. Fill with hot water (65-70°C) for 30-60 minutes
2. Drain and inspect for leaks
3. Optionally, "break in" with a sacrificial wine (lesser wine for first fill)
4. Some winemakers rinse with tartaric acid solution (3 g/L)

**For spirits barrels**:
1. Fill with hot water (70°C) to swell staves
2. Leave 20-30 minutes
3. Drain completely before filling with spirit
4. Never leave water standing (bacterial risk)

### Topping Up Protocol (Wine)

Evaporation creates ullage (headspace), which increases oxidation risk. Topping up replaces lost volume.

**Frequency**:
- First 3 months: Every 2 weeks
- Months 3-12: Monthly
- Year 2+: Every 6-8 weeks

**Procedure**:
1. Prepare topping wine (same wine, or similar quality)
2. Use a clean, sanitized pitcher or tank
3. Remove bung, fill to within 1-2cm of bung hole
4. Replace bung firmly
5. Record date and volume added

**Note**: Each topping adds approximately 1 mg/L oxygen. The upper 20-30cm of wine experiences 20-30 mV higher oxidation-reduction potential than the bulk.

### Topping Up for Spirits

Spirits barrels are typically **NOT topped up**. The ullage and evaporation are considered part of the maturation process:
- Oxygen ingress through ullage fuels essential oxidation reactions
- Concentration of flavors occurs as volume decreases
- Temperature cycling causes spirit to move in and out of wood

### Racking Protocol (Wine)

Racking aerates wine (2-5 mg/L oxygen) and removes sediment.

**Effects of racking**:
- Oxidation-reduction potential increases 50-100 mV immediately
- Returns to baseline in 15-20 days
- Oxygen consumed in 8-10 days

**Schedule for barrel-aged red wine**:
| Timing | Purpose |
|--------|---------|
| Post-MLF | First racking into barrels |
| 3 months | Rack off gross lees |
| 6 months | Optional rack if heavy sediment |
| 12 months | Rack before second winter |
| 18-24 months | Pre-bottling rack |

**Racking procedure**:
1. Move wine to destination vessel using gravity or gentle pump
2. Minimize splashing (closed racking for delicate wines)
3. Add 2-3 g/hL SO₂ after racking to protect against oxidation
4. Monitor free SO₂ levels

### Bung Position and Type

**Wine barrels**:
- Store bung-side-up during active fermentation (easy access)
- Store bung-at-2-o'clock (bilge position) for aging (keeps bung wet, better seal)
- Silicon bungs: Good seal, easy to remove
- Wood bungs: Traditional, prone to drying
- Glass bungs: Allow inspection, heavy

**Spirits barrels**:
- Store bung-up (traditional)
- Char/wood bungs typical

### Oxygen via Bunghole

Regardless of barrel age, approximately **0.5 mg O₂/L/year** enters via the bunghole, depending on bung type. The effect is most noticeable in the top 20cm of wine.

## Barrel Rotation Schedules

### Wine Barrel Program (24-Month Example)

For a winery aging red wines in barrels:

**Year 1 barrels (new)**:
- Use for best lots, highest-extraction wines
- Expect strong oak influence
- Blend carefully (new oak can dominate)

**Year 2 barrels (1-year-old)**:
- Moderate extraction
- Good for mid-tier wines
- 18-24 month aging cycles

**Year 3 barrels (2-year-old)**:
- Low extraction
- Extended aging for softer influence
- Consider for second wines or extended programs

**Year 4+ barrels (neutral)**:
- Minimal oak extraction
- Use for oxidative aging without oak flavor
- Microoxygenation benefits remain
- Retire or convert to spirits/beer use

**Annual rotation**:
1. Purchase new barrels (20-30% of program annually)
2. Move best lots to new wood
3. Cascade previous year's barrels down
4. Retire oldest barrels (4-5 years for wine)

### Spirits Barrel Rotation

**Bourbon** (new charred oak required by law):
- Single fill only for straight bourbon
- Used barrels go to Scotch, Irish, tequila producers
- Typical aging: 4-8 years

**Scotch** (ex-bourbon or ex-sherry):
- First-fill ex-bourbon: Strongest influence, 8-12 years
- Refill ex-bourbon: Moderate influence, 10-15 years
- Finish casks: 6-24 months in special wood (sherry, port, wine)

**Rum/Brandy**:
- Often use ex-bourbon barrels
- May refill multiple times
- Some producers use solera systems

### Solera System

The solera system provides consistent aged product by fractional blending:

**Structure** (example 4-tier solera):
```
Criadera 3 (youngest) ← New wine enters here
     ↓ (partial transfer)
Criadera 2
     ↓ (partial transfer)
Criadera 1
     ↓ (partial transfer)
Solera (oldest) → Product withdrawn here
```

**Protocol**:
1. Withdraw 20-30% from solera level for bottling
2. Replenish solera from Criadera 1
3. Replenish Criadera 1 from Criadera 2
4. Continue up the chain
5. Add new wine to top criadera

**Average age calculation**: A 4-tier solera with 25% annual withdrawal produces product with an average age of ~10 years after the system matures.

## Oak Alternatives

When barrels aren't practical, alternatives can provide oak character (though not identical oxygen ingress).

### Oak Products Comparison

| Product | Surface Area | Extraction Time | Best For |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|----------|
| Chips | Very high | 2-4 weeks | Quick flavor addition |
| Cubes | High | 4-8 weeks | Moderate extraction |
| Spirals | High | 6-10 weeks | Semi-controlled release |
| Staves | Moderate | 2-4 months | Barrel-like profile |
| Barrel segments | Low | 4-6 months | Extended contact |

### Oak Alternative Protocol

1. **Select toast level**: Light (citrus/vanilla), Medium (toast/caramel), Heavy (smoke/char)
2. **Sanitize**: Quick steam or brief sulfite soak (don't waterlog)
3. **Dose rate**:
   - Chips: 2-4 g/L
   - Cubes/spirals: 1-2 g/L
   - Staves: Per manufacturer guidelines
4. **Contact time**: Taste weekly; remove when desired character achieved
5. **Consider micro-oxygenation**: Oak alternatives lack natural oxygen transfer

### Micro-oxygenation (MOX)

MOX mimics barrel oxygen ingress for wines in tank with oak alternatives.

**Typical rates**:
- Wine aging: 1-2 mL O₂/L/month at 15°C
- Post-MLF development: 2-3 mL O₂/L/month

**Research finding** (Oberholster et al., 2014): MOX with oak chips or staves can approximate 6-month barrel aging sensory characteristics in new American or French oak, though polymeric pigment development differs.

## Alternative Wood Species (Non-Oak)

While oak dominates commercial barrel aging, several other wood species are used in craft brewing, distilling, and winemaking. Each brings a distinct chemical profile and requires different handling. None are direct substitutes for oak — treat them as flavor tools in their own right.

### Overview Comparison

| Wood | Tannin Level | Key Flavor Notes | Primary Use Cases |
|------|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Cherry** | Low–Medium | Almond, marzipan, dried cherry, subtle smoke | Beer, whiskey finishing, fruit brandy |
| **Apple** | Low | Baked apple, vanilla, sweet spice, honey | Cider aging, apple brandy, beer |
| **Pear** | Very Low | Delicate floral, pear blossom, subtle sweetness | Pear brandy (Williams), cider, light ale |
| **Walnut** | High | Bitter walnut, earthy, dark spice, leather | Spirits finishing (short contact only), nut liqueurs |

---

### Cherry Wood (Kirschholz)

**Species**: Primarily *Prunus avium* (wild/sweet cherry) and *Prunus serotina* (black cherry, North America)

**Chemical profile**:
- **Benzaldehyde**: The dominant volatile — delivers the characteristic almond/marzipan note. Formed from amygdalin degradation during wood drying and toasting.
- **p-Anisaldehyde**: Contributes sweet, anise-like, floral dimension.
- **Coumarin**: Vanilla-adjacent, sweet, hay-like note (low concentration).
- **Tannins**: Condensed tannins lower than oak; softer mouthfeel, less astringency.
- **Phenolic acids**: Lower ellagitannin content than oak; extraction is gentler.

**Flavor contribution**: Almond, marzipan, dried cherry, faint sweet smoke. Bridges well to malt character (toffee, caramel) and high-proof spirits. Medium-toast enhances benzaldehyde; heavy-toast introduces smoke and suppresses fruit notes.

**Practical notes**:
- Available as cubes, spirals, and chips from specialty suppliers
- Dose rate: 1–2 g/L (beer/wine), 1.5–3 g/L (spirits finishing)
- Contact time: 2–4 weeks for beer; 4–8 weeks for spirits
- Toast level: Medium recommended as starting point
- Works well in: barleywine, imperial stout, dark rum finishing, fruit brandy, cherry Hefeweizen
- Japanese craft distillers (notably Venture Whisky / Ichiro's Malt) have used cherry barrels alongside Mizunara for distinctive domestic expression

**Caution**: Amygdalin in raw, unprocessed cherry wood can yield trace hydrogen cyanide during fermentation — this risk is negligible in properly kiln-dried and toasted commercial wood products, but avoid using fresh/green cherry branches.

---

### Apple Wood (Apfelholz)

**Species**: *Malus domestica* and wild apple (*Malus sylvestris*)

**Chemical profile**:
- **Vanillin**: Present but lower than oak; gentle vanilla background.
- **Malic acid derivatives**: Apple wood retains associations with malic acid metabolism; contributes soft, rounded acidity impression.
- **Furfural**: Moderate levels from hemicellulose; caramel, bread crust notes.
- **Phenolics**: Low ellagitannin and condensed tannin content — very soft extraction.
- **Volatile esters**: Contribute light fruity, floral top notes.

**Flavor contribution**: Baked apple, honey, soft vanilla, sweet spice. Delicate and easily overwhelmed by strong base spirits — best suited to lighter styles or as finishing addition.

**Practical notes**:
- Dose rate: 1–2 g/L
- Contact time: 2–6 weeks (taste frequently — integration is quick)
- Toast level: Light to Medium; heavy toast strips fruit character and adds generic smoke
- Works well in: cider (obviously), apple brandy / Calvados-style spirits, farmhouse ales, golden ales, witbier, light lagers
- Traditional Norman and Breton producers occasionally age cider in apple wood vessels; effect is subtle and cumulative
- American craft cideries increasingly experiment with apple wood alternatives for "estate" character

**Note on cider**: Apple wood aging of cider can introduce a circular "apple on apple" quality that either integrates beautifully or reads as flat. Evaluate after 2 weeks before committing to longer contact.

---

### Pear Wood (Birnenholz)

**Species**: *Pyrus communis* (common pear), *Pyrus pyrifolia* (Asian pear)

**Chemical profile**:
- Among the most delicate of fruit woods — tannin and phenolic extraction is very low.
- **Floral esters**: Geraniol, linalool-adjacent compounds; jasmine, pear blossom impressions.
- **Furfural/5-HMF**: Moderate; contributes light caramel/marzipan.
- **Vanillin**: Trace levels; barely perceptible vanilla.
- **Tannins**: Minimal — essentially no structural tannin contribution. Does not replace oak tannin in wine.

**Flavor contribution**: Pear blossom, light floral, subtle sweetness, mild caramel. Extremely gentle — noticeable primarily in neutral or lightly flavored base products.

**Practical notes**:
- Dose rate: 2–4 g/L (higher than oak alternatives to achieve perceptible effect)
- Contact time: 4–10 weeks
- Toast level: Light; heavier toasting quickly dominates and masks the delicate pear character
- Works well in: Williams/Bartlett pear brandy (Williamine-style), perry, Belgian saison, delicate white wines
- Williams Christ pear brandy from Alsace and Switzerland is occasionally rested in pear wood vessels — the effect is primarily textural softening rather than flavor addition
- Low tannin means pear wood cannot substitute for oak in tannin-dependent structures (red wine aging, tannic whiskey maturation)

---

### Walnut Wood (Walnussholz)

**Species**: *Juglans regia* (European/Persian walnut), *Juglans nigra* (black walnut, North America)

**Chemical profile**:
- **Juglone** (5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone): The defining compound of walnut wood. Bitter, astringent, slightly medicinal. Antimicrobial properties. *Juglans nigra* contains significantly more juglone than *J. regia* — use European walnut for aging applications.
- **Ellagitannins**: High content — comparable to or exceeding oak. Strong astringency potential.
- **Plumbagin**: Minor naphthoquinone; bitter, harsh.
- **Vanillin / syringaldehyde**: Present after toasting; standard lignin-degradation products.
- **Walnut-specific phenolics**: Contribute earthy, dark, leather, nutty, and forest-floor notes.

**Flavor contribution**: Bitter walnut, dark earthiness, leather, dried spice, forest floor. At high doses or long contact: harsh bitterness and astringency. At low doses and short contact: complexity and depth, particularly in dark spirits.

**Practical notes**:
- **Use sparingly** — walnut is the most aggressive alternative wood listed here
- Dose rate: 0.5–1.5 g/L (start at the lower end)
- Contact time: 1–3 weeks only; re-taste every 7 days
- Toast level: Medium to Medium-Plus — toasting converts some juglone precursors and softens harshness; light toast is not recommended
- Use *Juglans regia* (European walnut) over *Juglans nigra* wherever possible
- Works well in: peated whisky finishing (complements smoky/earthy profile), dark rum, amaro base spirits, walnut liqueur production (nocino), imperial stout
- Traditional use: Nocino (Italian green walnut liqueur) uses unripe walnut husks/shells, not wood — a distinct product. Some small Italian and French producers age grappa or eau-de-vie with brief walnut wood contact for bitterness balance.

**Phytotoxicity note**: Juglone is toxic to many plants. This has no relevance to human consumption at the concentrations found in properly used wood products — it contributes bitterness, not danger. However, it is responsible for walnut wood's antimicrobial properties, which can slow or inhibit fermentation if used during active yeast activity. **Add walnut wood only after primary fermentation is complete.**

---

### Blending Non-Oak Woods with Oak

Non-oak woods rarely replace oak entirely; they work best as a complement:

| Combination | Ratio (non-oak : oak) | Effect |
|-------------|----------------------|--------|
| Cherry + French oak | 1:2 | Marzipan over vanilla/spice base |
| Apple + neutral oak | 1:1 | Soft fruit + structural tannin |
| Pear + light oak | 1:2 | Floral lift without oak dominance |
| Walnut + American oak | 1:4 | Earthy bitterness over coconut/vanilla |

Evaluate at 2-week intervals. Remove the non-oak component first if balance tips toward bitterness or astringency — oak extraction continues after the alternative wood is removed.

## Mellowing mit Holzwürfeln und -stäben (Accelerated Maturation)

Mellowing bezeichnet den Prozess, mit dem ein frischer, scharfer Rohbrand oder junger Geist seinen „Spirit-Bite" verliert — die stechende Schärfe, die Fuseligkeit, den brennenden Abgang. Barrels leisten das über Jahre; Holzwürfel und Stäbe beschleunigen denselben Prozess auf Wochen.

### Warum Rohbrand scharf schmeckt — und was Holz dagegen tut

Vier Hauptursachen für „Spritigkeit":

1. **Fuselalkohle** (Propanol, Butanol, Amylalkohol): Bei der Destillation entstanden, schmecken sie brennend, lösungsmittelartig, beißend.
2. **Acetaldehyd und andere Aldehyde**: Stechend, fruchtig-scharf, unreif.
3. **Freie Fettsäuren**: Fettig, ranzig, kratzig im Abgang.
4. **Rohe Ethanolschärfe**: Unmodifiziertes Ethanol ohne Ester- oder Tannin-Pufferung.

Holz wirkt auf allen vier Ebenen:

| Mechanismus | Was passiert | Ergebnis |
|------------|-------------|---------|
| **Tannin-Aldehyd-Bindung** | Ellagitannine und kondensierte Tannine reagieren mit Acetaldehyd und Furfural; Bindungsprodukte sind organoleptisch weich | Aldehyd-Schärfe verschwindet |
| **Esterifikation** | Holzsäuren + Fuselalkohle → Ester (langsam, aber nachweisbar) | Fuselcharakter wird fruchtiger/weicher |
| **Adsorption** | Getoastete/gekohlte Holzoberfläche adsorbiert polare Verbindungen inkl. Fuselalkohle | Direkte physikalische Reduktion von Fuseln |
| **Mikro-Oxygenierung** | Sauerstoff aus dem porösen Holz oxidiert Ethanol → Acetaldehyd → Essigsäure → Ester | Runder, komplexer, weniger „roh" |
| **Polymerisierung** | Phenole verbinden sich zu längerkettigen Molekülen mit weicherem Mundgefühl | Strukturelle Glättung |

**Wichtig**: Kein Holz „entfernt" Alkohol — der ABV bleibt konstant. Mellowing verändert die Wahrnehmung von Alkohol durch chemische und organoleptische Modifikation des Umfelds.

### Würfel vs. Stäbe: Wann welches Format?

| Eigenschaft | Würfel (10–15 mm) | Stäbe / Dominos (20–30 × 60–100 mm) |
|------------|-------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Oberfläche/Volumen** | Sehr hoch | Mittel |
| **Extraktionsgeschwindigkeit** | Schnell (2–4 Wochen messbar) | Langsamer (4–10 Wochen) |
| **Kontrolle** | Einfach: Handvoll entnehmen zum Kosten | Weniger feinjustierbar |
| **Risiko der Überextraktion** | Höher bei langer Kontaktzeit | Geringer — gibt Puffer |
| **Mellowing-Effizienz** | Hoch bei kurzen Zeiträumen | Gleichmäßiger Verlauf über Zeit |
| **Empfehlung für** | Schneller Test, Experimentieren | Geplante Langzeitreifung |

**Faustregel**: Für Mellowing von Rohbrand (3–8 Wochen) → **Würfel**. Für kontinuierliche Nachreifung eines bereits voragerten Destillats (8–16 Wochen) → **Stäbe**.

---

### Mellowing-Protokoll: Die vier Holzsorten

#### Kirsche (Kirschholzwürfel / -stäbe)

**Stärken beim Mellowing**: Benzaldehyd überlagert Aldehyd-Schärfe aromatisch; niedrige Tannine glätten, ohne zu adstringieren. Ideal für mittelstarke Brände (40–50 % ABV).

| Parameter | Empfehlung |
|-----------|-----------|
| Format | Würfel (Medium-Toast) |
| Dosis | 1,5–2,5 g/L |
| Kontaktzeit | 3–6 Wochen |
| Zwischenkostung | Woche 2, Woche 4, Woche 6 |
| Ziel-ABV-Bereich | 40–55 % |
| Kombination | + 0,5–1 g/L leichte Eichenwürfel für tannin-Rückgrat |

**Verlauf**: Woche 2 — Aldehydschärfe merklich reduziert, Marzipan-Note beginnt; Woche 4 — runder Abgang, Kirsche integriert; Woche 6 — Gefahr der Überextraktion (Bitterkeit). Lieber früher herausnehmen.

**Passt zu**: Obstbrand (Kirsche, Pflaume), Whiskey, weißem Rum, Grappa.

---

#### Apfel (Apfelholzwürfel / -stäbe)

**Stärken beim Mellowing**: Sehr sanft. Kein dominanter Aromastempel — Fuselreduktion durch Adsorption und Esterifikation, ohne das Grundbild zu verändern. Das „Neutral-Mellowing" unter den Obsthölzern.

| Parameter | Empfehlung |
|-----------|-----------|
| Format | Würfel (Light- bis Medium-Toast) |
| Dosis | 2–3 g/L |
| Kontaktzeit | 4–8 Wochen |
| Zwischenkostung | Woche 2, Woche 5, Woche 8 |
| Ziel-ABV-Bereich | 38–60 % |
| Kombination | Gut solo; oder + Birnenholz 1:1 für floralere Note |

**Verlauf**: Sanfter als Kirsche — Woche 4 erste wahrnehmbare Glättung, Woche 6–8 deutliches Mellowing. Überextraktionsrisiko niedrig: Apfelholz gibt kaum bittere Verbindungen ab.

**Passt zu**: Calvados-Stil, Apfelbrand, Cidre-Geist, Korn, Neutral-Destillat, Sake-Destillat.

---

#### Birne (Birnenholzwürfel / -stäbe)

**Stärken beim Mellowing**: Das delikateste der vier Hölzer. Kaum Tanninbeitrag → ideal wenn Struktur nicht erwünscht (leichte Geiste, florale Profile). Mellowing primär über Adsorption und Mikro-O₂, wenig über Tannin-Bindung.

| Parameter | Empfehlung |
|-----------|-----------|
| Format | Würfel (Light-Toast) |
| Dosis | 3–5 g/L (höher als andere, da niedriger Extrakt) |
| Kontaktzeit | 6–10 Wochen |
| Zwischenkostung | Woche 3, Woche 6, Woche 9 |
| Ziel-ABV-Bereich | 38–50 % |
| Kombination | + 1 g/L Kirschholz für aromatischen Akzent |

**Verlauf**: Langsam und linear. Keine dramatischen Geschmackssprünge — eher kontinuierliche Glättung. Für Trinker, die am Ende „Ich weiß nicht genau warum, aber es schmeckt runder" sagen sollen.

**Passt zu**: Williams-Birnengeist, Perry-Destillat, Pisco-Stil, Weißbier-Destillat, Obstler.

---

#### Walnuss (Walnussholzwürfel / -stäbe)

**Stärken beim Mellowing**: Hohe Ellagitannine binden Aldehyde sehr effizient — schnellste Aldehyd-Reduktion der vier Hölzer. **Aber**: Juglon bringt Bitterkeit, die schwer wieder herauszubekommen ist. Walnuss ist das schärfste Werkzeug hier — präzise dosieren.

| Parameter | Empfehlung |
|-----------|-----------|
| Format | Würfel (Medium-Toast — senkt Juglonaktivität) |
| Dosis | **0,5–1 g/L** (nicht überschreiten) |
| Kontaktzeit | **1–3 Wochen** (wöchentlich kosten!) |
| Zwischenkostung | Tag 7, Tag 14, Tag 21 |
| Ziel-ABV-Bereich | 45–65 % (höherer Alkohol puffert Bitterkeit besser) |
| Kombination | Nie solo als alleiniges Mellowing-Holz — immer mit Eiche oder Kirsche |

**Verlauf**: Woche 1 — Aldehyde merklich reduziert, kein Fehlaroma. Woche 2 — Erdig-nussige Note erkennbar, Abgang weicher. Woche 3 — Bitterkeit beginnt zu dominieren → **Herausnehmen**. Bei Überschreitung: Bitter lässt sich nur durch Verdünnung oder Blending korrigieren.

**Passt zu**: Getorfter Whiskey, dunkler Rum, Amaro-Basis, Williamsbirne (kleine Menge für Struktur).

### Kombiniertes Mellowing-Protokoll (Praxisbeispiel)

Beispiel: 5 Liter frischer Obstbrand, 52 % ABV, typisch scharf/fuselig.

**Ansatz**:
```
Phase 1 (Woche 1–2):
  Walnuss-Würfel  0,8 g/L  →  4 g gesamt
  → Schnelle Aldehyd-Bindung

Phase 2 (ab Woche 2, nach Entnahme Walnuss):
  Kirsch-Würfel   2,0 g/L  → 10 g gesamt
  Apfel-Würfel    1,5 g/L  →  7,5 g gesamt
  → Aromatische Glättung, Fuselabbau, Esterbildung

Kostung:
  Tag 7:   Walnuss-Effekt evaluieren, ggf. früher herausnehmen
  Tag 14:  Walnuss raus, Kirsche + Apfel rein
  Tag 28:  Erste Vollkostung Phase 2
  Tag 42:  Entscheidung: weiter oder abfüllen
```

**Zielparameter (Nasenprobe)**:
- Kein stechender Acetaldehydgeruch mehr
- Ethanol tritt in den Hintergrund (riecht weniger „nackt")
- Frucht/Holznoten integriert, nicht dominant

---

### Häufige Fehler beim Holz-Mellowing

| Fehler | Konsequenz | Lösung |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| Zu hohe Dosis Walnuss | Irreversible Bitterkeit | Start mit 0,5 g/L, steigern nach Kostung |
| Holz zu früh in aktive Gärung | Juglon/Tannine hemmen Hefe | Nur nach Gärungsende zugeben |
| Gleiche Kontaktzeit bei verschiedenen Hölzern | Überextraktion bei Kirsche, Unterextraktion bei Birne | Hölzer getrennt oder gestaffelt herausnehmen |
| Kein Toasting der Würfel | Rohe Gerbsäure, grüne/bittere Fehlaromen | Ausschließlich getoastete Würfel kaufen oder kurz im Ofen (160 °C, 20 min) rösten |
| Zu niedriger ABV (<38 %) | Schlechte Tannin-Löslichkeit, Mikrobenstabilität unsicher | Auf min. 40 % verdünnen nach Destillation |

## Barrel Care and Storage

### Storing Empty Barrels

Empty barrels are vulnerable to drying and microbial contamination.

**Short-term (1-4 weeks)**:
1. Rinse thoroughly with hot water
2. Drain completely
3. Burn sulfur stick inside (1-2 sticks per barrel)
4. Seal with bung
5. Store in cool, humid location

**Long-term (1+ months)**:
1. Clean with hot water and citric acid solution (10 g/L)
2. Rinse thoroughly
3. Drain and dry partially
4. Burn sulfur (repeat monthly)
5. Store bung-down on racks in humid cellar
6. Check monthly; re-sulfur as needed

**Sulfur burning protocol**:
- Use barrel sulfur sticks or wicks
- Insert through bung hole
- Let burn 5-10 seconds inside barrel
- Remove, seal immediately
- Creates SO₂ atmosphere that prevents mold/bacteria

### Barrel Reconditioning

When barrels show contamination or excessive neutrality:

**For neutral barrels (no contamination)**:
- Shave interior to expose fresh wood
- Re-toast or re-char
- Returns some extraction potential

**For contaminated barrels**:
- Hot water/steam cleaning (180°F+)
- Citric acid soak (10 g/L, 24 hours)
- Sodium percarbonate cleaning
- Ozone treatment
- If Brett/VA persistent: convert to vinegar production or discard

### Barrel Retirement Criteria

Consider retiring wine barrels when:
- 4-5 fills for wine (5-6 years typical)
- Extraction testing shows <10% of new barrel
- Persistent off-flavors despite cleaning
- Structural issues (leaks, damaged staves)

Retired wine barrels can be:
- Sold to distillers/brewers
- Used for neutral aging
- Converted to planters/furniture
- Used for vinegar production

## Oxygen and Oxidation-Reduction Summary

### Total Oxygen Exposure During Winemaking

| Phase | O₂ Added (mg/L) |
|-------|-----------------|
| **Vatting** | |
| Pre-fermentation maceration | 5-8 |
| Alcoholic fermentation (pump-overs) | 30-60 |
| Post-fermentation vatting | 1-4 |
| Running off | 4-6 |
| **Aging (16 months)** | |
| New barrel aging | 27-60 |
| Used barrel aging | 15-20 |
| Racking (per rack) | 5-25 |
| Topping up (total) | 3-12 |
| Filtration | 4-8 |
| Bottling | 3 |
| **Total (new barrel program)** | 54-121 |
| **Total (used barrel program)** | 42-81 |

*Source: Handbook of Enology, 2006*

### Oxidation-Reduction Potential Targets

| Wine State | EH (mV) | Notes |
|------------|---------|-------|
| Post-fermentation | 150-200 | Highly reduced |
| During barrel aging | 200-250 | Moderate oxidation |
| After racking | 250-350 | Temporary spike |
| Pre-bottling | 180-220 | Slightly reduced |
| Bottle aging | Decreasing | Approaches minimum |

**Warning signs**:
- EH < 150 mV: Reduction risk (H₂S, mercaptans)
- EH > 350 mV: Excessive oxidation risk

## Troubleshooting Barrel Issues

| Problem | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---------|--------------|----------|
| Excessive oak | New barrel, long contact | Rack to neutral vessel; blend |
| Leaking barrel | Dried staves, failed stave/head | Soak to swell; repair if possible |
| VA (vinegar) developing | Acetobacter contamination | Add SO₂; rack immediately; quarantine barrel |
| Brett character | Brettanomyces contamination | SO₂ addition; may need to discard barrel (see wine-science.md IBM protocol) |
| Musty/moldy smell | TCA contamination | Test barrel with model wine; discard if contaminated |
| Stuck fermentation in barrel | Cool temperature, low nutrients | Warm gently; add nutrients; inoculate |
| Oxidized character | Insufficient topping; bad bung | Assess damage; may be irreversible |
