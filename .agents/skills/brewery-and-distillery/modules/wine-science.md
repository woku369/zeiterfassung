## 1. Yeasts and Bacteria in Winemaking

### The Role of Yeasts

Yeasts are responsible for converting grape sugar into alcohol. Without yeasts, all we would have is grape juice. Of the estimated 1,000 or so volatile flavor compounds in wine, at least 400 are produced by yeast.

**Key concept**: Microbes have short generation times and are fiercely competitive. If conditions favor one yeast or bacterium slightly over others, it will rapidly outpace the competition. Winemakers should ensure their musts give a competitive advantage to the desired organisms.

### Native Yeasts on Grapes

Native yeast populations found on grape skins include:

| Yeast Species | Notes |
|---------------|-------|
| *Kloeckera apiculata* / *Hanseniaspora uvarum* | Most common on grapes |
| *Metschnikowia pulcherrima* | Present in lesser amounts |
| *Candida famata*, *C. stellata* | Minor populations |
| *Pichia* species | Various strains present |
| *Brettanomyces* | Potential spoilage organism |

**Important**: The bloom on grape skins is not composed of yeast—it's a waxlike scaly material. Yeast populations on intact grapes are relatively low.

*Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (the main wine yeast) is rare on grape skins but becomes dominant during fermentation. Modern sequencing confirms it exists in vineyards and on grape skins, spreading via aerial transmission and insect transfer.

### Spontaneous vs. Inoculated Fermentation

**Spontaneous (wild/indigenous) fermentation**:
- Uses native yeasts present on grapes and in the winery
- Initial inoculum is low, so fermentation starts slowly
- Risk: Spoilage organisms (like *Acetobacter*) may establish before desirable yeasts
- Succession: Apiculate yeasts dominate initially → various *Candida* species → *S. cerevisiae* takes over at 4-6% alcohol
- May involve 20-30 different strains
- Advantages: Fuller palate structure, slower/cooler ferments preserve aromatics, cost savings

**Inoculated fermentation**:
- Uses cultured strains of *S. cerevisiae*
- More predictable and controlled
- Winemaker can select strain characteristics
- Estimated 80% of wine worldwide uses inoculated ferments

**Pied de cuve** (starter fermentation):
- Pick grapes before main harvest, crush, let ferment
- Use this to inoculate main fermentation
- Provides insurance against problems
- Allows selecting a ferment that smells nice

### Wild Yeast Research Insights

Research by Matthew Goddard (New Zealand) has shown:

1. **Local populations exist**: Strains carrying out spontaneous fermentations derive from the local environment and appear unique compared to strains elsewhere
2. **Sources**: Soil, bark, flowers, and insect transfer (especially bees)
3. **Barrel transfer**: *S. cerevisiae* has been found in new French oak barrels—possible international transfer mechanism
4. **Not escaped commercial strains**: Local populations are genetically distinct from commercial yeasts

**Implication for terroir**: Different regions harbor different yeast populations, so using region-specific yeasts means wines more faithfully reflect sense of place.

### Malolactic Fermentation (MLF)

**What it is**: Bacterial conversion of malic acid to lactic acid

**Organisms**: Primarily *Oenococcus oeni* (formerly *Leuconostoc oenos*)

**Effects**:
- Reduces acidity (malic acid is sharper than lactic)
- Softens the wine's mouthfeel
- Can add buttery notes (diacetyl production)
- Increases microbial stability

**When used**:
- Virtually all red wines
- Some white wines (esp. oaked Chardonnay)
- Avoided in crisp whites where acidity is prized

**Timing**: Usually after primary fermentation completes

## 2. Wine Flavor Chemistry

### Understanding Wine Flavor

Wine flavor is a multimodal sensation combining:
- **Gustation** (taste): From taste buds in tongue and mouth
- **Olfaction** (smell): From olfactory receptors in nasal cavity
- **Touch**: Feel of liquid in mouth
- **Vision**: Visual cues shape perception
- **Context/experience**: What we know affects what we perceive

**Key insight**: The "taste" of wine is a property of the taster based on chemical composition—not an intrinsic property of the wine itself. We are all living in our own unique taste world due to differences in olfactory receptor repertoires.

### Wine's Chemical Composition

Wine contains an estimated 800-1,000 volatile molecules. However, complexity goes beyond the number of compounds:

1. **Individual variation**: People differ in sensitivity to specific compounds (ageusia for taste, anosmia for smell)
2. **Interactions**: Masking effects (one compound interferes with perception of another) and synergistic effects (perception created by combination)

### Ferreira's Classification of Wine Odorants

Vicente Ferreira (Universidad Zaragoza) categorizes wine flavor compounds:

**1. Basal Wine Aroma (20 compounds in all wines)**:

- Higher alcohols (butyric, isoamylic, hexylic, phenylethylic)
- Acids (acetic, butyric, hexanoic, octanoic, isovalerianic)
- Ethyl esters from fatty acids
- Acetates and compounds like diacetyl
- Ethanol

**Ethanol's influence**: As alcohol rises from 11% to 14%, volatile compound recovery decreases. At 14.5%, fruity aromas from esters can be totally masked.

**2. Contributory Compounds (16 compounds, below perception threshold but synergistic)**:

- Volatile phenols (guaiacol, eugenol, isoeugenol)
- Ethyl esters
- Fatty acids
- Acetates of higher alcohols
- Branched aldehydes
- Vanillin and derivatives
- Aliphatic γ-lactones

**3. Impact Compounds (distinctive varietal aromas)**:

| Compound | Character | Found In |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| 2-methoxy-3-isobutylpyrazine (MIBP) | Green pepper, grassy | Sauvignon Blanc, Cab Sauv |
| Monoterpenes (linalool) | Floral, citrus | Muscat varieties |
| Rose-cis-oxide | Sweet, flowery, rose | Gewürztraminer |
| Rotundone | Black pepper | Syrah (20% of people cannot smell this) |
| 3MH, 3MHA, 4MMP | Tropical, passion fruit, grapefruit | Sauvignon Blanc |

### The Nonvolatile Matrix

The nonvolatile matrix (wine constituents without aromatic character) strongly influences how aromatic molecules are perceived.

**Key experiment** (Ferreira): When aromatics from a white wine were placed in a red wine matrix, tasters started using red fruit descriptors. The matrix determines how we interpret the smell.

**Implication**: Wine flavor cannot be understood by volatile composition alone—the interaction between volatiles and the wine matrix is critical.

## 3. Sulfur Dioxide (SO₂)

### Why SO₂ is Essential

Sulfur dioxide acts as a "chemical custodian" in two ways:

1. **Antioxidant protection**: Prevents oxidation (most important function)
2. **Antimicrobial action**: Prevents growth of unwanted yeasts and bacteria

Peter Godden (AWRI) calls it a "magical substance" because it works at very low concentrations (typically <150 mg/L in most wines).

### Free vs. Bound SO₂

When SO₂ is added to wine:
- Some dissolves as **free SO₂** (active form)
- Some reacts with wine components to become **bound SO₂** (inactive)

**Bound by**: Ethanal (acetaldehyde), ketonic acids, sugars, dicarbonyl compounds

**Key measurement**: Ratio of free to bound SO₂ (higher is better)

**Molecular SO₂** is the truly active fraction:
- Target: ~0.8 mg/L molecular SO₂ in white wines
- Requires 15-40 mg/L free SO₂ (depending on pH)

### The Critical Role of pH

pH dramatically affects SO₂ effectiveness:

| pH | % Molecular SO₂ |
|----|-----------------|
| 2.9 | 7.5% |
| 3.0 | 6.1% |
| 3.2 | 3.9% |
| 3.4 | 2.5% |
| 3.6 | 1.6% |
| 3.8 | 1.0% |

**Double benefit of low pH**: More SO₂ in useful free form AND it works more effectively.

### How SO₂ Protects Wine

**Against enzymatic oxidation** (in must):
- Oxidases from damaged/rotten grapes
- Clean grapes = less SO₂ needed

**Against chemical oxidation** (in wine):
- Oxygen made reactive by iron and copper ions
- Creates quinones and hydrogen peroxide
- SO₂ binds these, limiting damage
- Also binds oxidation products like acetaldehyde

**Why whites need more than reds**: Red wines have polyphenolics that provide natural oxidation defense.

### Best Practice: Getting the Ratio Right

**Start with healthy grapes**: Rotten grapes have more SO₂-binding compounds

**Three critical addition points**:
1. At crushing
2. At end of MLF (or alcoholic fermentation if no MLF)
3. At bottling

**Key insight**: For the same total addition, fewer large doses are more effective than many small additions (ensures free SO₂ reaches effective levels).

**Winemaking practices that improve ratio**:
- Clean grapes with minimal fungal damage
- Careful racking, fining, filtration
- General winery cleanliness

### Health Considerations and Regulations

**Regulatory limits**:
- EU: 160 mg/L (dry reds) to 400 mg/L (botrytized)
- Australia: 250 mg/L (dry) to 350 mg/L (sweet)
- USA: Similar; "Contains Sulfites" required if >10 mg/L

**WHO recommended daily allowance**: 0.7 mg/kg bodyweight
- 70 kg person = 49 mg/day max
- Half bottle at 150 mg/L = 56 mg (exceeds RDA)

**Note**: Dried fruits contain ~10x more sulfites than wine.

### Natural Wine and Zero-SO₂ Winemaking

**Why attempt it**:
- Consumer demand for "additive-free"
- Winemaker ideology (wine as natural product)

**Challenges**:
- Even with no additions, yeast naturally produces 5-15 mg/L SO₂
- Requires extremely clean grapes and perfect winemaking
- Higher risk of oxidation and spoilage

**Jacques Néauport method**: Carbonic maceration under very cold conditions

## 4. Wine Faults

### Philosophy of Faults

**Key insight**: Beauty is not the absence of flaws. Small traces of what might otherwise be faults at higher levels can bring out the beauty of wine (the Japanese concept of wabi sabi).

**The presence of a fault compound does not make a wine faulty.** The wine must be evaluated as a whole.

Some faults are always faults (cork taint), others are context-dependent.

### Cork Taint (TCA)

**Cause**: 2,4,6-trichloroanisole (TCA) from microbial contamination of cork

**Character**: Musty, wet cardboard, suppressed fruit

**Current rate**: Estimated 1-3% of cork-sealed bottles (improved from 5-10%+ historically)

**Detection**: Humans can detect TCA at extremely low levels

**Alternatives**:
- Screwcaps (various liner options for oxygen transmission)
- DIAM (technical cork with supercritical CO₂ cleaning)
- Nomacorc (synthetic)

### Brettanomyces (Brett)

**Cause**: Metabolites from *Brettanomyces bruxellensis* yeast, especially 4-ethylphenol and 4-ethylguaiacol

**Character**:
- Low levels: Savory, spicy, medicinal (Band-Aid)
- High levels: Barnyard, horse saddle, animal sheds
- Also: Metallic edge, drier texture (strips residual sugars)

**Primarily a red wine problem** (whites have protective low pH and fewer Brett food sources)

**Controversy**: Some wines with detectable Brett taste good; others are ruined. Context matters.

### Oxidation

**Character**: Stale, cardboard, papery (whites); nutty, sherry-like, browning (reds)

**Prevention**:
- Minimize oxygen pickup during transfers
- Proper sulfite levels
- Reduce headspace in vessels

**Note**: Oxidation and volatile acidity often occur together

### Volatile Acidity (VA)

**Cause**: Acetic acid from *Acetobacter* (and some MLF bacteria), which require oxygen

**Character**: Vinegar-like sharpness

**Critical for reds**: Keep cap wet during fermentation to prevent *Acetobacter* growth

**At low levels**: Can add pleasant lift; at high levels, off-putting

### Reduction

**What it is**: Volatile sulfur compounds (VSC) produced by yeast

**Not the opposite of oxidation**: A wine can be both oxidized and reduced simultaneously

**Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)**: Rotten egg smell—often transient during fermentation

**More complex VSC**:
- Can create range of aromas
- Change mouthfeel
- Sometimes positive: matchstick/struck flint in Chardonnay (methional)
- Polyfunctional thiols in Sauvignon Blanc are technically VSC

**Management**: Racking off lees, adequate yeast nutrients, avoiding fermentation stress

### Mouse Taint

**Cause**: Tetrahydropyridines from lactic acid bacteria, especially under low-sulfite conditions

**Character**: Mousy, caged mice, metallic aftertaste (perceived on the finish, after swallowing)

**Rising incidence**: Increase correlated with growth of natural wine movement

**Note**: Some people cannot perceive mouse taint at all

## 5. The Science of Sweet Wines

### Strategies for Sweet Wine Production

1. **Stop fermentation early**: Before yeast converts all sugar
2. **High initial sugar**: From very ripe or concentrated grapes
3. **Concentrate/add sugar**: Through various methods

### Noble Rot (Botrytis cinerea)

**What it is**: Infection of ripe grapes by *Botrytis cinerea* fungus

**Conditions needed**:
- Morning mists followed by dry afternoons
- Healthy, ripe, metabolically active grapes

**Effects on grapes**:
- Shrivels berries, concentrating sugar and acid
- Alters grape metabolism
- Creates unique flavor precursors (polyfunctional thiols)

**Key insight** (Denis Dubourdieu): "Infection is not only about concentration—it is stimulation of the production of aroma precursors by the pulp." Grapes must be metabolically active when infected.

### Tokaji (Hungary)

**Method**:
1. Selectively pick shriveled aszú berries in multiple passes (6-10 kg/person/day)
2. Store aszú berries (high sugar/acid preserves them)
3. Collect free-run juice for Essencia (600-900 g/L sugar)
4. Macerate aszú berries in fermenting wine (12-60 hours)
5. Press slowly, add pressings back
6. Continue fermentation to desired balance

**Puttonyos scale**: Grades of sweetness (now 4, 5, or 6 puttonyos based on residual sugar)

**Key grapes**: Furmint (~65%), Hárslevelű (~20%)

### Sauternes (Bordeaux)

**Method**:
- Harvest when significant botrytis present (often one pass)
- Press directly (releases spore clouds)
- Ferment in barrel
- New oak complements rich flavors

**Flavor compounds identified**:
- Polyfunctional thiols (3MH)
- α-terpineol, sotolon
- Fermentation alcohols and esters
- Woody characters (from oak)

**Denis Dubourdieu's insight**: Fast ripening and fast invasion by botrytis = best thiols (passion fruit, mango, grapefruit). Mature grapes simply concentrate (honey, citrus).

### German Sweet Wines

**Advantages of Mosel + Riesling**:
- Naturally high acidity balances sweetness
- Cool climate allows extended hang time

**Sweetness levels**:
- Beerenauslese (BA): Selected nobly rotted berries
- Trockenbeerenauslese (TBA): Dried nobly rotted berries (most concentrated)

## 6. Wine Science Calculations and Measurements

### Sugar to Alcohol Conversion

**Rule of thumb**: ~17 g/L sugar = 1% alcohol

Example: Grapes at 240 g/L sugar → potential ~14% alcohol

### pH and Acidity

**Typical wine pH range**: 3.0-3.8

| Wine Type | Typical pH |
|-----------|------------|
| Crisp white | 3.0-3.3 |
| Fuller white | 3.3-3.5 |
| Red wine | 3.4-3.7 |
| Low-acid red | 3.6-3.8 |

**Total acidity (TA)**: Measured in g/L tartaric acid equivalent
- White wines: 6-9 g/L
- Red wines: 5-7 g/L

### Sulfite Calculations

**Molecular SO₂ calculation**:

```
Molecular SO₂ (mg/L) = Free SO₂ × (1 / (1 + 10^(pH - 1.81)))
```

**Target molecular SO₂**: 0.5-0.8 mg/L for antimicrobial effect

**Quick reference for free SO₂ needed**:
| pH | Free SO₂ for 0.8 mg/L molecular |
|----|--------------------------------|
| 3.0 | 13 mg/L |
| 3.2 | 21 mg/L |
| 3.4 | 32 mg/L |
| 3.6 | 50 mg/L |
| 3.8 | 80 mg/L |

### Volatile Acidity

**Threshold of concern**: >0.6 g/L acetic acid
**Legal limits vary**: Typically 1.2 g/L max

## 7. Practical Winemaking Guidance

### Pre-Fermentation

1. **Assess grape quality**: Reject damaged/moldy fruit (or sort meticulously for botrytis wines)
2. **Add SO₂**: ~50 mg/L at crush for wild yeast control
3. **Manage temperature**: Cool grapes if necessary for controlled extraction
4. **Test**: Brix/potential alcohol, pH, TA

### Primary Fermentation

**Temperature ranges**:
- White wines: 54-64°F (12-18°C) for aromatic preservation
- Red wines: 77-86°F (25-30°C) for extraction

**Monitoring**:
- Daily Brix/SG readings
- Temperature tracking
- Cap management for reds (punch down or pump over 2x daily)

### Post-Fermentation

**MLF decision** (for reds and some whites):
- Inoculate with *O. oeni* if desired
- Maintain warmth (64-68°F)
- Test for completion (paper chromatography or enzymatic test)

**Pressing decisions** (for reds):
- Time on skins: 7-21 days depending on extraction goals
- Press wine may be kept separate or blended

### Aging and Maturation

**Oak options**:
- New barrels: Strong vanilla, toast, tannin
- Used barrels: Softer influence
- Oak alternatives: Chips, staves, spirals (cost-effective)

**Racking schedule**:
- First rack: After settling (2-4 weeks post-fermentation)
- Subsequent: Every 2-3 months to reduce lees contact

### Fining and Stabilization

**Cold stabilization**: Chill to 28-32°F for 2+ weeks to precipitate tartrates

**Protein stability** (whites): Bentonite fining

**Sulfite addition**: Before bottling, aim for adequate free SO₂ based on pH

### Bottling

**Pre-bottling checklist**:
- Wine clear and stable
- Appropriate free SO₂ level
- pH and TA measured
- Sterile filtration if desired (0.45 micron)

## 8. Advanced Oak Science

*From Clark Smith's "Postmodern Winemaking" (2014)*

### The Seven Functions of Oak

Oak in winemaking serves far more purposes than simple flavor addition:

| Function | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **1. Coextraction** | Oak absorbs harsh tannins during fermentation, later releases smoother compounds |
| **2. Antioxidative Power** | Oak tannins (ellagitannins) protect wine from oxidation |
| **3. Sweetness** | Oak lactones add perceived sweetness without sugar |
| **4. Framing** | Vanilla and related compounds provide aromatic "frame" for fruit |
| **5. Structure** | Tannin polymerization creates mouthfeel and aging potential |
| **6. Curing Aromatics** | Time-release of bound aromatics during aging |
| **7. Micro-oxygenation** | Slow oxygen ingress through barrel staves |

### Oak Vessels vs. Alternatives

| Format | Character | Best For |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **New barrels** | Intense extraction, micro-ox, prestige | Age-worthy reds, oaked Chardonnay |
| **Used barrels (1-3 yrs)** | Gentler extraction, aging benefits | Most reds needing oak |
| **Neutral barrels (4+ yrs)** | Micro-ox only, no flavor | Delicate wines needing aging |
| **Staves** | Good surface area, no micro-ox | Cost-effective oak character |
| **Chips** | Fast extraction, less elegant | Inexpensive wines, quick turnaround |
| **Spirals/cubes** | Moderate extraction | Compromise solution |

### Oak Selection Factors

**Forest origin matters:**
- French (Tronçais, Allier, Vosges): Finer grain, subtle
- American (Missouri, Minnesota): Wider grain, more lactones
- Hungarian/Slavonian: Between French and American

**Toasting levels:**
- Light toast: More raw wood, lactones
- Medium toast: Balanced vanilla, spice
- Medium-plus: Caramel, mocha notes
- Heavy toast: Smoky, espresso, diminished fruit

**Key compounds from toasting:**
- Vanillin (vanilla)
- Eugenol (clove)
- Furfural (butterscotch)
- Guaiacol (smoke)
- Whiskey lactones (coconut)

## 9. Graceful Aging: Building Wine Structure

*From Clark Smith's "Postmodern Winemaking" (2014)*

### The Structure-Longevity Relationship

Wines age gracefully when they have:

1. **Adequate acidity** — Preserves freshness, antimicrobial
2. **Tannin structure** — Phenolic backbone that evolves
3. **Reductive strength** — Ability to consume oxygen
4. **Aromatic complexity** — Compounds that transform interestingly

### Phenolic Polymerization

Young wines contain small, reactive tannin molecules that:
- Taste astringent and harsh
- React aggressively with proteins (mouth-drying)
- Are unstable

With time and micro-oxygenation:
- Tannins polymerize (chain together)
- Larger polymers taste softer
- Eventually become too large and precipitate
- Color shifts from purple-red to garnet to brick

### Managing Reductive Strength

**Reductive strength** = wine's capacity to absorb oxygen without damage

**Building blocks:**
- Anthocyanins (color pigments)
- Tannins (from skins, seeds, oak)
- Sulfites
- Other phenolics

**The paradox:** Wines need some oxygen to develop complexity, but too much destroys them. The art is matching oxygen exposure to wine's capacity.

### Practical Implications

**For home winemakers:**
- Extended maceration builds structure
- Racking schedule affects oxygen exposure
- Cork vs. screwcap = different aging trajectories
- Bottle age recommendations vary by structure

**Wine types by aging potential:**

| Structure Level | Aging Potential | Examples |
|-----------------|-----------------|----------|
| Low tannin, low acid | 1-3 years | Light reds, rosé |
| Moderate tannin | 5-10 years | Village Burgundy, Merlot |
| High tannin + acid | 10-25+ years | Barolo, Bordeaux, Hermitage |
| Fortified | Decades+ | Port, Madeira |

## 10. Integrated Brettanomyces Management

*From Clark Smith's "Postmodern Winemaking" (2014)*

### Understanding Brett

**Brettanomyces bruxellensis** is a yeast that:
- Lives in barrels, winery surfaces, fruit flies
- Feeds on trace sugars *Saccharomyces* leaves behind
- Produces 4-ethylphenol (4-EP) and 4-ethylguaiacol (4-EG)
- Creates "bretty" character: barnyard, leather, Band-Aid, horse

### Detection Thresholds

| Compound | Sensory Threshold | Character |
|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| 4-ethylphenol | 400-600 μg/L | Barnyard, medicinal |
| 4-ethylguaiacol | 50-100 μg/L | Smoky, bacon, clove |

**Ratio matters:** 4-EP:4-EG ratio affects overall impression. Higher 4-EG ratios smell more pleasant (smoky vs. barnyard).

### Risk Factors

**High risk:**
- High pH (>3.7)
- Low sulfite levels
- Residual sugar (even 0.5 g/L)
- Warm storage
- Old barrels
- Extended barrel aging

**Lower risk:**
- pH < 3.5
- Adequate free SO₂ (>35 mg/L)
- Clean, newer barrels
- Cool cellar (55°F/13°C)

### Clark Smith's IBM Protocol

1. **Accept some Brett** — Low levels can add complexity
2. **Manage, don't panic** — Monitor and adjust
3. **Control pH** — Lower pH = less active Brett
4. **Maintain SO₂** — Keep molecular SO₂ above 0.5 mg/L
5. **Sterile filtration** — 0.45 μm removes most Brett cells
6. **Avoid mixing** — Don't contaminate clean lots

### When Brett Becomes a Fault

**Low levels (< 200 μg/L 4-EP):**
- Often undetectable
- May add complexity
- Terroir expression to some

**Moderate levels (200-600 μg/L):**
- Divisive — some like it, some don't
- Can complement certain styles

**High levels (> 600 μg/L):**
- Dominates wine character
- Generally considered faulty
- Difficult to remediate

### Remediation Options

| Method | Effectiveness | Concerns |
|--------|---------------|----------|
| Sterile filtration | Removes cells, not compounds | Must catch early |
| Activated charcoal | Removes 4-EP/4-EG | Also strips desirable compounds |
| Reverse osmosis | Can remove Brett compounds | Expensive, alters wine |
| Blending | Dilutes Brett character | Need clean blending lots |
