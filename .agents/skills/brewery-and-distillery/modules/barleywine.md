## S1 Style Definition

Barleywine is the biggest beer you can brew. It is defined by excess — of malt, of hops, of alcohol, of time. The name itself acknowledges that this ale reaches into wine territory in strength and complexity.

### Parameters

| Parameter | English Barleywine (BJCP 17D) | American Barleywine (BJCP 22C) | Allen & Cantwell Minimum |
|-----------|------------------------------|-------------------------------|--------------------------|
| **OG** | 1.080-1.120 | 1.080-1.120 | 1.080+ (preferably 1.090+) |
| **FG** | 1.018-1.030 | 1.016-1.030 | 1.020-1.032 |
| **ABV** | 8-12% | 8-12% | 8.5%+ |
| **IBU** | 35-70 | 50-100 | 50-100 |
| **SRM** | 8-22 | 10-19 | 8-22 (must not be opaque) |

### Non-Negotiable Characteristics (per Allen & Cantwell)

1. **Starting gravity at least 1.080** — anything less is not a barleywine, regardless of what the label says.
2. **All barley malt** (or barley malt with sugar/extract to augment gravity). Wheat, oats, and other grains "spin formulation off into a separate world."
3. **Not opaque** — "held to the light, it may be dim or russet, even burnt in color, but it must be seen through." This distinguishes barleywine from imperial stout.
4. **Intended for aging** — a barleywine should benefit from at least 90 days of aging, and the best improve over 1-10+ years.

## S2 Regional Families

### English Barleywine

The defining example was **Bass No. 1** (ceased production 1995, OG > 1.100). Modern examples: Fuller's Golden Pride, Lee's Harvest Ale, J.W. Lees Barley Wine, Gale's Prize Old Ale.

**Character**: Mellowness, softness. Toasted sugar or caramel from extended boil or sugar additions. Moderate alcohol presence. Fruity ester array: apricot, cherry, coconut, pineapple, dried banana. Generally not aggressively bitter. Hop aroma diminishes with age. Effects of slight oxidation can be positive — sherry-like, vinous.

**Process hallmarks**:
- Parti-gyle brewing common (Fuller's Golden Pride → London Pride → Chiswick Bitter from successive runnings)
- Extended aging traditional
- Often darker than American versions (melanoidin stability from dark malts enables better long-term aging)
- Moderate hopping; balance tilts toward malt

### American Barleywine — Northeast

The "mid-Atlantic" of barleywine. Carries most elements of the British approach — age, mellowness, moderate hopping — but freed from British tax constraints, produces barleywines of commensurate size to the biggest West Coast examples. Often labeled "old ale" rather than barleywine. More direct relation to English old ale tradition. First East Coast brewpubs were started by Englishmen, so philosophically more conservative.

### American Barleywine — Northwest (West Coast)

Defined by **Anchor Old Foghorn** (1975, the first American craft barleywine) and especially **Sierra Nevada Bigfoot**. Bigfoot is the stylistic pole: aggressively hopped, cleaner/drier than English examples, higher attenuation, more prominent alcohol. Hop character is paramount.

**Why so hoppy**: Proximity to one of the world's great hop-growing regions (Yakima Valley, Willamette Valley). Seattle and Portland have extremely soft water, allowing massive hop loads without excessive vegetal astringency.

**Character**: Pale to amber. High bitterness (often 65-100 IBU). Aggressive hop aroma (American varieties: Cascade, Centennial, Chinook, Columbus). Cleaner fermentation. Higher attenuation = more prominent alcohol, drier palate. Still malt-forward, but hops provide equal dramatic weight.

## S3 The Malt Bill

### Principle: Less Is More

"A less complex grain bill is often better" for barleywine. The sheer quantity of pale malt (2-4x a normal brew) already provides enormous malt character. Specialty malts are magnified by the extended boil and long conditioning; what seems right in a pale ale will be overpowering in a barleywine.

### Pale Malt (85-100% of grain bill)

**English pale ale malt is the best choice** — particularly **Maris Otter**. It is highly modified, low in protein, with a rounder, more complex, biscuity/bready flavor than American malts. American two-row can produce excellent barleywines (Sierra Nevada Bigfoot uses American malt) but will be "lighter, less complex, crisper, and cleaner."

The choice of pale malt — English vs. American — is the single biggest flavor decision in the grain bill.

**Six-row should be avoided** — too much protein, too little extract.

### Specialty Malts (0-15% of grain bill)

**Maximum 15% total specialty/colored malts.** Dark malts should be **under 2%** of total grain bill.

Extended boiling already produces significant caramelization and melanoidin formation from the pale malt alone — up to 25% of total color can come from a 90-minute boil of an all-pale malt beer. Adding heavy specialty malt on top of this natural caramelization results in muddled, cloyingly sweet beer.

**Recommended specialty malts** (use 1-2 types, not more):

| Malt | Max % | Purpose |
|------|-------|---------|
| **Crystal/Caramel (40-80L)** | 5-8% | Color (orange/red), sweetness, body. Use sparingly. |
| **Munich** | 5-10% | Malt flavor depth, slight color. Works well with American pale malt to add complexity. |
| **Dextrin/CaraPils** | 3-5% | Body, head retention. No significant flavor contribution. |
| **CaraMunich** | 3-5% | Rich malt flavor, moderate color. |
| **Belgian Biscuit/Aromatic** | 3-5% | Bready/toasty accent. |
| **Dark malts (Chocolate, Roasted Barley)** | < 2% | Color only. Excessive dark malt makes an imperial stout, not a barleywine. |

### Adjuncts (0-15% of fermentables)

Sugar or malt extract can augment gravity, especially for homebrewers with equipment limitations.

- **Malt extract**: Keep to 40% or less of total fermentables (60% for homebrew). At these levels, minimal flavor impact.
- **Non-malt sugars** (cane, corn, beet, rice): Keep to 15% or less. These provide fermentables without adding flavor but can thin the beer if overused.
- **Dark brown sugar, treacle, golden syrup**: Traditional British adjuncts. Provide interesting flavors (molasses, caramel, toffee) within the 15% limit.

## S4 Hops

### Bittering Philosophy

Two camps exist:

| | English Approach | American (NW) Approach |
|---|---|---|
| **IBU range** | 35-70 | 65-100+ |
| **Balance** | Malt-forward, hops as structure | Hops as co-equal dramatic element |
| **Aging arc** | Less hoppy ages better long-term (4+ years) | Highly hopped ages better short-term (1-3 years), then swings out of balance |

The brewer must decide which camp they fall into, then plan accordingly. "The gradual marrying of the mercurial hop and the stolid barleycorn is a key to the brewer's art."

### Bittering Hops

Use **high-alpha hops** (8-16% AA) for bittering. Advantages:
1. Less hop material = less wort loss to absorption
2. Less vegetal flavor from boiling enormous quantities of low-alpha hops
3. Less risk of clogging the heat exchanger

**Recommended bittering hops**: Magnum (15-16% AA, low cohumulone = soft bitterness), Warrior, Columbus, Galena, Chinook, Nugget.

**Cohumulone note**: Higher cohumulone = harsher bitterness. Magnum is the exception to the "high AA = high cohumulone" rule — it has very low cohumulone and soft bitterness.

**High-gravity utilization adjustment**: Hop utilization decreases as gravity increases. Add 1-4% more boiling hops than normal:
- 1.080 wort: +1%
- 1.100+ wort: +4%

**Timing**: Do not boil hops for more than 90 minutes. Extended boiling beyond 90 minutes extracts harsh, unpleasant bitterness and can hydrolyze iso-alpha-acids into non-bitter humulinic acid (reducing effectiveness). If your boil exceeds 90 minutes (common for barleywine gravity-building), delay hop additions accordingly.

### Finishing / Aroma Hops

No rules — depends on the barleywine you want to make.

| Style | Appropriate Finishing Hops |
|-------|--------------------------|
| Traditional English | East Kent Golding, Fuggle, Challenger, Northdown |
| American | Cascade, Centennial, Chinook, Columbus, Liberty |
| Continental accent | Hallertau, Tettnang, Saaz (not traditional but not forbidden) |

Finishing hops can be added at end-of-boil, in the whirlpool, in a hop back, or dry-hopped in the fermenter.

**Aging consideration**: Hop aroma and flavor are the first things to go as a beer ages. Iso-alpha-acid bitterness also diminishes over time. Plan for this: a beer perceived as aggressively hoppy when fresh may be perfectly balanced at 1-2 years.

## S5 Yeast

"If malt is the backbone of a barley wine, then yeast is its soul."

### Selection Criteria (in order of priority)

1. **Flavor / ester profile** — Use an ale yeast. Top-fermenting yeast produces the "heady and powerful flavors and aromas" that define barleywine. Lager and champagne yeasts should only be used as last-resort fermentation rescues.

2. **Alcohol tolerance** — Must handle at least 8% ABV, ideally 10-12%+. Most ale yeasts handle 8-9%. Some go to 12-14%.

3. **Attenuation** — Medium-high to high attenuator. FG should be 20-28% of OG (e.g., OG 1.100 → FG 1.020-1.028). A good attenuator that also handles the complex sugars (maltotriose, trisaccharides) will produce a cleaner, less cloyingly sweet beer while leaving enough residual body.

4. **Flocculation** — Moderate to moderate-high preferred. Highly flocculant yeasts may drop out before finishing fermentation, requiring rousing.

### Recommended Yeast Strains

| Strain | Attenuation | Alcohol Tolerance | Character | Notes |
|--------|------------|-------------------|-----------|-------|
| **WLP002 / Wyeast 1968 (London ESB)** | Medium-high | ~9-10% | Malt-accentuating, fruity esters, slightly sweet finish | Classic English barleywine yeast. May need rousing (highly flocculant). |
| **WLP007 (Dry English)** | High | ~10%+ | Clean, dry, attenuative | Good for higher-attenuated American-leaning versions. |
| **WLP001 / Safale US-05 (California Ale)** | High | ~9-10% | Clean, neutral | Clean canvas for hop character. Standard American choice. |
| **WLP013 / Wyeast 1028 (London Ale)** | Medium | ~9% | Complex, malty, fruity | Traditional London character. May stall on very high-gravity worts. |
| **Wyeast 1056 (American Ale)** | High | ~9-10% | Clean, well-balanced | Same strain as WLP001/US-05. Reliable. |

### Dual-Yeast Strategy

Some brewers pitch an ale yeast for primary flavor development, then pitch a second more alcohol-tolerant strain when fermentation slows:
- Primary: English ale yeast for flavor and ester complexity
- Secondary: More attenuative ale yeast, or as last resort, champagne/wine yeast

**Caution with champagne yeast**: Over-attenuates (can digest complex sugars needed for body/flavor), can produce non-beer flavors, and may cause over-carbonation during cold conditioning.

### Pitching Rate

**1 million cells per mL per degree Plato.** For a 23 Plato (1.092) barleywine: 23 million cells/mL. This is a lot of yeast.

Practical guidance:
- **Professional**: Use yeast from a recent healthy fermentation. Pitch 1.5-2x the normal volume/weight.
- **Homebrew**: Make a **large starter** (2-3L on a stir plate) or pitch 2-3 packets of dry yeast. Always overpitch rather than underpitch for high-gravity beers. Have backup yeast ready in case fermentation stalls.
- **Never use a yeast's first generation** if possible. Harvest from a recent fermentation for maximum vigor.

### Oxygenation

"You cannot put too much air, or oxygen, into the wort" at ale fermentation temperatures. Wort simply cannot hold enough dissolved O2 to be problematic at these temperatures. Target 10-14 ppm dissolved oxygen. Underoxygenation is one of the most common causes of stuck barleywine fermentations.

- **O2 stone**: 1.5 L/min for the duration of knockout
- **Air (if no pure O2)**: 3.0 L/min, sterile-filtered
- Aerate in-line during transfer to fermenter for maximum contact time

## S6 Water Chemistry

### General Principle

There is no single "classic" barleywine water. High-gravity beers were brewed successfully across England in areas with radically different water chemistry — from soft London to extremely hard Burton-upon-Trent. "If your water is potable, you can brew with it."

### Maximum Mineral Levels for Brewing Water

| Mineral | Maximum |
|---------|---------|
| Bicarbonate | < 50 ppm (remove excess by pre-boiling) |
| Calcium | < 270 ppm |
| Chloride | < 100 ppm |
| Iron | < 1 ppm |
| Magnesium | < 100 ppm |
| Sulfate | < 650 ppm |
| Sodium | (via NaCl) < 300 ppm |

### Burton-upon-Trent Water (Historical Reference)

If there were a "classic" barleywine water, it would be Burton's — home of Bass No. 1.

| Mineral | Amount |
|---------|--------|
| Sulfate | 640 ppm |
| Calcium | 270 ppm |
| Bicarbonate | 200 ppm |
| Carbonate | 140 ppm |
| Magnesium | 60 ppm |
| Chloride | 40 ppm |
| Sodium | 30 ppm |

This is extremely hard, sulfate-dominant water. The high sulfate accentuates bitterness and hop character — ideal for Burton pale ales and, historically, for Bass No. 1's hop-forward profile. **This profile is NOT recommended for modern malt-forward barleywines** — it would push bitterness perception too high.

### Modern Water Recommendations by Style

| Barleywine Style | SO4:Cl Ratio | Character |
|-----------------|-------------|-----------|
| **English (malt-forward)** | 1:1 to 1.5:1 | Balanced to slightly malt-favoring. Moderate sulfate supports hop structure without dominating. |
| **American NW (hop-forward)** | 2:1 to 3:1 | Sulfate-forward. Accentuates hop bitterness and crispness. |
| **Full Burton treatment** | 4:1+ | Extreme sulfate. Only for very heavily hopped versions. Historical, not necessarily recommended. |

### Suggested Modern Profiles

**English Barleywine (balanced)**:

| Ion | Target (ppm) |
|-----|-------------|
| Ca | 75-100 |
| Mg | 10-15 |
| Na | 20-30 |
| SO4 | 80-120 |
| Cl | 60-80 |
| HCO3 | 50-100 (buffer for any specialty malt acidity) |

**American Barleywine (hop-forward)**:

| Ion | Target (ppm) |
|-----|-------------|
| Ca | 75-100 |
| Mg | 10-15 |
| Na | 15-25 |
| SO4 | 150-200 |
| Cl | 50-75 |
| HCO3 | 25-50 |

### pH Targets

- **Mash pH**: 5.2-5.4
- **Kettle pH**: 5.2-5.4 (favors protein coagulation, hop utilization, and reduces harsh bitterness; optimum for protein coagulation is 5.2)

## S7 The Brewing Process

### Milling

**Do not overcrush.** This is the single most important mechanical step. Barleywines use 2-4x normal grain, creating much deeper mash beds and higher screen pressures. Overcrushed malt → compacted mash bed → stuck runoff → "living hell."

If homebrewing and buying pre-crushed grain, inspect the crush. Better too coarse than too fine.

### Mashing

**Single infusion mash is best for barleywine.** Decoction and step mashing reduce the mash to finer particles and beat air out — air that helps keep the mash bed buoyant. With the massive grain bills involved, maintaining mash bed structure is critical.

**Temperature**: 145-152F (63-67C).
- **145-146F (63C)** for maximum fermentability (60+ minutes). Maximizes beta-amylase activity, producing the most fermentable wort. Recommended for barleywine to avoid excessive sweetness. With the massive malt load, there will be plenty of unfermentable dextrins for body even at lower mash temps.
- **Above 152F (67C)**: Wort will likely be "overly sweet and dextrinous, resulting in a poorly attenuated and unbalanced beer."

**Water ratio**: Medium to medium-thick, ~0.9 qt/lb (slightly less than 1 qt/lb).

**Do not overfill the mash tun.** Do not exceed 2-3x normal mash bed depth. Lowering yield is preferable to overfilling and dealing with stuck mashes. Make multiple brews to fill a fermenter if necessary.

### Runoff and Sparging

- **Start slowly.** Do not pull too hard — will compact the mash bed.
- **Begin sparging** while wort level is still ~1 inch above the grain bed. Do not let the grain bed go dry.
- **Sparge water**: 170F (77C). Keep depth above grain to less than 2 inches (excess weight compresses the bed).
- **Monitor gravity** throughout runoff. Typical progression: starts at 17-20 Plato (1.068-1.080), rises to mid-low 20s (1.084-1.092), then falls off. Stop runoff when runnings drop to 9-12 Plato (1.036-1.048).
- **Collect last runnings** separately to make a "small beer" — traditional practice dating back centuries. Free mild beer from the remaining fermentables.

**If the mash sets** (stuck sparge):
1. **Underlet**: Force hot water up from below the false bottom to lift the mash bed. Fast but dilutes gravity.
2. **Stir the entire mash** down to the screens. Effective but requires recirculation to clarity and risks hot-side aeration.
3. **Wait it out**: If flow is merely slow (not stopped), patience may be the best option.
4. **Prevention**: Don't overcrush. Don't runoff too fast. Don't overload the mash tun. Limit dark malt and wheat.

### The Boil

**Duration: 2 to 2.5 hours recommended.** Longer than normal for several reasons:
- **Gravity concentration** via evaporation. At 8-12% evaporation per hour, a 2-hour boil can increase gravity by 5+ Plato.
- **Enhanced protein coagulation** (hot break) — critical for long-term stability.
- **Caramelization and melanoidin formation** — contributes malty sweetness and color.
- **DMS elimination** — driven off by vigorous boiling.

**Maximum 3-3.5 hours total boil.** Beyond this, aggregated protein precipitates can disassociate. Do not boil with hops for more than 90 minutes.

**Boil must be vigorous and rolling.** Aim for 8-12% evaporation per hour. Good kettles achieve 10-11%. The rolling action facilitates protein coagulation (albumin fractions aggregate around steam bubbles).

**Evaporation rate calculation**: (Pre-boil volume - Post-boil volume) / Pre-boil volume / Hours = Rate per hour. Example: (37 bbl - 32 bbl) / 37 / 1.5 hours = 9% per hour.

**Hop addition timing**: If boiling longer than 90 minutes, delay first hop addition so that hops boil for no more than 90 minutes total.

**Kettle pH**: Target 5.2-5.4. At pH 5.2, protein coagulation is optimal and hop utilization is favored without harsh bitterness. Add gypsum to lower pH if needed.

### Color Formation

Extended boiling increases color through sugar caramelization and melanoidin (nonenzymatic browning) formation. Up to 25% of total color in an all-pale-malt beer can come from a 90-minute boil. Implications:
- **Reduce specialty malt percentages** compared to lower-gravity beers
- **For pale barleywines**: Minimize dark malt, keep boil to 2 hours, and accept that some color pickup is inevitable

### Fermentation

**Temperature**: 66-72F (19-22C). This range balances ester production (wanted, in moderation) against fusel alcohol production (unwanted). Do not combine high gravity AND high temperature — "can create some pretty strange-tasting beers."

**Timeline**:
1. **Active fermentation**: 7-14 days. May begin vigorously then slow to a steady pace by day 2-3. This is normal — do not panic.
2. **Diacetyl rest**: 7-10 days at or near fermentation temperature (58-70F). Critical for barleywine — high-gravity fermentations produce more diacetyl. Without this rest, pronounced butterscotch off-flavors.
3. **Rousing**: If fermentation slows or stalls, rouse the yeast. Methods: blow CO2 through the bottom valve, pump from bottom to top at low speed, or physically agitate the fermenter (homebrew scale). Keeps yeast in suspension, speeds attenuation.

**Monitoring**: FG should be 20-28% of OG. For OG 1.100: FG 1.020-1.028.

**Have backup yeast ready.** If primary yeast stalls, pitch fresh, more alcohol-tolerant yeast immediately.

### Cold Aging

**Duration: 1-3 months minimum, up to 1 year before packaging.**

After diacetyl rest:
1. Chill to 32-52F (0-11C).
2. Wait ~1 week, then remove precipitated yeast (draw off bottom of conical, or rack off yeast cake).
3. Continue aging. The yeast contact during early cold conditioning is beneficial for flavor development; extended contact risks autolysis ("yeast bite" — meaty, yeasty off-flavors).

**Autolysis risk**: After the first week of cold aging, remove as much yeast sediment as possible. Autolysis releases enzymes that decompose cell walls, leaking unpleasant flavors into the beer. Colder temperatures slow autolysis.

### Filtration (Optional)

Allen & Cantwell's position: "We don't think [filtration is] necessary. We believe the proper amount of cold storage can leave a beer in better shape than filtration." Filtration strips color, flavor, body, hop bitterness, and hop aroma along with haze-forming proteins and yeast.

If you must filter, use a **pad filter with open/high-porosity pads** (e.g., Schenk HS 6000) to remove large haze-forming protein complexes without stripping desirable constituents.

### Packaging

**Kegs are superior** for oxygen control — larger package = proportionately smaller headspace = lower O2-to-beer ratio.

**Always bottle at least some** for tracking aging progress, sharing, and vintage comparison.

**Carbonation**: Moderate to low. 1.8-2.4 volumes CO2 typical. Barleywine should not be highly carbonated — over-carbonation makes it feel thin and masks the rich, viscous character. Some carbonation is desirable; completely flat beer is not.

**Bottle conditioning** requires special care for high-gravity beers:
- Yeast may be exhausted after months of fermentation/conditioning. Dose with **fresh, active yeast** along with priming sugar.
- Use actively fermenting wort (10 Plato / 1.040, ~25 million cells/mL) as primer: 4-6 L per US barrel.
- Warm-condition bottles at 65-70F for 30+ days.
- Then move to cellar temperature (48-55F).
- Wait **minimum 120 days** after priming before drinking.

### Cellaring (Post-Packaging Aging)

**Cellar temperature (48-56F / 9-13C) is optimal** for aging packaged barleywine. Multiple brewers and researchers confirm that cellar-temperature aging produces rounder, more robust, more cohesive flavors than refrigerator aging. However, above 60F, chemical reactions accelerate too much.

**Recommended approach**: Decrease temperature as aging length increases. Example: 50F for the first 1-2 years, then move to cooler storage for extended aging.

**Aging timeline**:
- **90 days minimum** before drinking
- **1-2 years**: Most barleywines show marked improvement
- **2-4 years**: Highly hopped versions peak, then begin to swing out of balance as hops fade
- **4-8 years**: Less hoppy, malt-forward versions continue to improve
- **6-10+ years**: Some exceptional examples remain excellent. Higher OG = more graceful aging.

**What changes with age**:
- Hop bitterness diminishes
- Hop aroma fades (first thing to go)
- Malt character evolves (caramel → toffee → sherry-like)
- Fruity esters develop and shift
- Slight oxidation (at controlled levels) produces sherry, port, dried fruit notes — these can be positive
- Alcohol integrates and becomes less "hot"

**Vintage dating is essential.** Mark every bottle and keg.

## S8 Wood Aging

### Historical Context

For centuries, all beer was made and aged in wood. Wood contributes its own flavors, harbors microflora (Lactobacillus, Brettanomyces), and retains flavors from previously packaged beverages. Modern breweries have mostly abandoned unlined wood because it cannot be fully sanitized.

### Oak

Allen & Cantwell found success with **French limousin oak chips** — "only French oak was mild enough to use without overpowering." Dose: **0.5-1 oz per US barrel** (very sparingly).

**American oak** is more aggressive: higher vanillin and lactone extraction. Use less.

### Barrel Aging

Used bourbon/whiskey, port, wine, or sherry barrels. The Allen & Cantwell book notes early experiments with Jim Beam barrel-aged imperial stout at Goose Island that were "awesome."

**Barrel aging guidance for barleywine**:
- **Bourbon barrels**: Vanilla, caramel, coconut (from American oak lactone), whiskey warmth. 3-12 months contact.
- **Port/wine barrels**: Vinous, dried fruit, tannic structure. 6-18 months.
- **Sherry barrels**: Oxidative, nutty, raisin. Exceptional for English-style barleywine. 6-24 months.

### Cherry Wood (Cross-Reference: Midnight Fluff Clone)

See the Midnight Fluff clone recipe's cherry wood addendum for detailed analysis of cherry wood's unique benzaldehyde and p-anisaldehyde contributions. Cherry wood could provide an interesting dimension to barleywine — the almond/marzipan note from benzaldehyde would bridge to the toffee/caramel malt character. Medium-toast cherry cubes, 2-3 oz per 5 gallons, 2-4 weeks.

## S9 Troubleshooting

### Stuck Fermentation (FG too high)

**Diagnostic sequence** (from Allen & Cantwell):
1. **Temperature**: Is the beer too cold? Warm to 70F (21C).
2. **Yeast in suspension?** If yeast has dropped, rouse it (CO2 from bottom, pumping, manual agitation).
3. **Oxygenation**: Was there enough O2 at pitching? If unsure, **add more oxygen to the fermenter**. "This sounds mad, but what other choices are left?" The high gravity ensures renewed fermentation will consume and scrub all O2.
4. **Alcohol tolerance**: If still not fermenting after 12-24 hours, the yeast strain may have reached its alcohol limit. Pitch a fresh, more alcohol-tolerant strain from a recent fermentation, with additional oxygen.

### Stuck Mash (See Mashing section above)

### Low Kettle Gravity

**Boil longer.** An extra 30-60 minutes at 8-12% evaporation/hour can make a significant difference. Just keep hops to ≤ 90 minutes boil time. Take periodic gravity readings during the boil to track progress.

### Too Hoppy

**Age it.** Even the most bitter beer will mellow in 3-4 years. Alternative: blend with a less hoppy beer (but this may just create twice as much mediocre beer).

### Cloudy Beer

Give it 3-6 more months of cold aging (≤ 52F). If still hazy: isinglass, clarigen finings, or (last resort) filtration. Cold aging is almost always the best option.

### Flat Bottle-Conditioned Beer

1. Wait another month — high-gravity beers can take months to carbonate in bottle.
2. If still flat: open each bottle and dose with a tiny amount of alcohol-tolerant yeast (a single grain of dry yeast per bottle). Be fanatically sanitary.

### Excessive Sweetness / Poor Attenuation

Likely causes:
- Mash temperature too high (> 152F)
- Insufficient pitching rate
- Inadequate oxygenation
- Yeast strain not alcohol-tolerant enough
- Too much specialty malt (especially crystal)

Remedies: Rouse, warm, re-oxygenate, pitch more attenuative/tolerant yeast.

## S11 Quick Reference Summary

```
BARLEYWINE BREWING CHECKLIST

GRAIN BILL:
  - 85-100% pale malt (Maris Otter ideal for English style)
  - 0-15% specialty malts (keep it simple — 1-2 types max)
  - < 2% dark malts (if any)
  - 0-15% adjunct sugar (if needed for gravity)

MASH:
  - Single infusion, 145-152F, 60+ min
  - Water ratio ~0.9 qt/lb
  - Do NOT overfill mash tun (2-3x normal bed depth max)
  - Do NOT overcush grain

BOIL:
  - 2-2.5 hours (max 3.5 hours)
  - Hops in for max 90 minutes
  - Target 8-12% evaporation/hour
  - High-alpha hops for bittering (Magnum recommended)
  - Adjust hop rate +1-4% for high gravity

FERMENTATION:
  - Pitch rate: 1M cells/mL/Plato (massive pitch)
  - Oxygenate aggressively (10-14 ppm)
  - 66-72F, 7-14 days
  - Diacetyl rest: 7-10 days at fermentation temp
  - Rouse yeast if fermentation slows
  - Have backup yeast ready

COLD AGING:
  - 32-52F, 1-3 months minimum (up to 1 year)
  - Remove yeast after first week to prevent autolysis

PACKAGING:
  - 1.8-2.4 vol CO2 (moderate-low carbonation)
  - Bottle condition with fresh yeast + priming sugar
  - Warm condition 30+ days, then cellar
  - Wait 120 days minimum before drinking

CELLARING:
  - 48-56F optimal
  - Most improve over 1-2 years
  - Highly hopped versions peak at 2-4 years
  - Malt-forward versions can age 6-10+ years
  - Vintage date everything
```
