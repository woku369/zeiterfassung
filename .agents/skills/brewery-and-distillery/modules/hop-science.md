# Hop Science

**Confidence**: High
**Last validated**: 2025-01-26

Comprehensive hop reference covering biology, chemistry, products, brewing techniques, variety profiles, and style pairing. Synthesized from *For The Love of Hops* (Hieronymus, 2012), *The Hops List* (Poel, 2018), and *The Book of Hops* (DiSorbo & Christiansen, 2021).

## §1 Hop Biology and Anatomy

### The Hop Plant

**Humulus lupulus** is a perennial climbing bine (not vine — bines climb by wrapping stems, vines use tendrils) in the family Cannabaceae, related to cannabis. Hops are dioecious: male and female flowers grow on separate plants. Only female flowers (cones/strobiles) are used in brewing.

**Growth characteristics:**
- Grows 15-25 feet per season from an established rootstock (crown)
- Rhizome-propagated (clonal) for commercial consistency
- Photoperiod-sensitive: requires 15+ hours of daylight to trigger flowering
- Best grown between 35-55° latitude (both hemispheres)
- Harvest: late August through September (Northern Hemisphere); February-March (Southern)

### Cone Anatomy

The hop cone consists of:
- **Bracts and bracteoles**: Petal-like structures forming the cone shape
- **Lupulin glands**: Yellow resinous glands at the base of bracteoles containing alpha acids, beta acids, and essential oils — the source of all brewing value
- **Strig**: Central stem of the cone

**Dried cone composition:**
| Component | Percentage |
|---|---|
| Cellulose (green matter) | 40-50% |
| Proteins and minerals | 10-25% |
| Water | 8-10% |
| Tannins and polyphenols | 3-5% |
| Alpha acids | 2-20% |
| Beta acids | 2-10% |
| Essential oils | 0.5-4% |
| Other acids, fats, simple sugars | 5-10% |

### Subspecies

Five landrace botanical varieties form the ancestral base for most cultivated hops. **Humulus lupulus var. neomexicanus** (wild American hop) has gained commercial interest — Medusa was the first commercially farmed neomexicanus variety, followed by Sabro, Talus, and Zappa. These contribute unique tropical, fruit, and herbal characteristics not found in European or standard American cultivars.

## §2 Hop Chemistry

### Alpha Acids (Humulones)

Alpha acids are the primary bittering compounds, residing in the soft resin of lupulin glands. They are not bitter in their native form — they must be **isomerized** (structurally rearranged) by boiling to become soluble and bitter.

**Three analogues:**
| Analogue | Typical Proportion | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Humulone** | 35-70% of total alpha | Primary analogue |
| **Cohumulone** | 20-55% of total alpha | Higher levels historically associated with harsher bitterness; modern research disputes this — the "cohumulone debate" |
| **Adhumulone** | 10-15% of total alpha | Minor analogue |

**Cohumulone**: Measured as a percentage of total alpha acids. Average is 25-30%. Low-cohumulone hops (e.g., Simcoe 15-20%, Sabro 20-24%) are prized for smoother bitterness. High-cohumulone hops (e.g., Topaz 47-53%, Cluster 36-42%) produce more assertive bitterness. However, Stan Hieronymus notes the debate is unresolved — some excellent bittering hops have high cohumulone.

**Alpha acid ranges by hop purpose:**
- Aroma hops: 2-8% AA
- Dual-purpose hops: 8-14% AA
- Bittering hops: 12-20+% AA

### Isomerization

When alpha acids are boiled, they undergo isomerization into **iso-alpha acids** — six distinct forms (cis and trans isomers of isohumulone, isocohumulone, and isoadhumulone). These are the compounds primarily responsible for beer bitterness.

**Key isomerization facts:**
- Requires sustained heat at or near boiling (100°C / 212°F)
- Rate slows dramatically below 88°C (190°F)
- Nearly stops below 82°C (180°F) — the basis for Parish Brewing's "sub iso" technique
- Maximum practical utilization ~35-40% (most alpha acids are never isomerized)
- Trans-iso-alpha acids degrade faster than cis forms, so bitterness fades over time

### Beta Acids (Lupulones)

Beta acids (lupulone, colupulone, adlupulone) have limited direct solubility in wort. Their brewing significance:
- **Antimicrobial**: Contribute to hop preservative effect
- **Oxidative bittering**: When oxidized during storage, beta acids produce mildly bitter hulupones — used intentionally in aged/lambic hops
- **Storability index**: Higher beta-to-alpha ratio generally indicates better storability

### Essential Oils

Hop oils are volatile aromatic compounds comprising 0.5-4% of cone weight. They are largely destroyed by boiling, so late additions and dry hopping are essential for aroma.

**Major terpene hydrocarbons:**

| Compound | Found in | Flavor/Aroma | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Myrcene** | Mango, lemongrass, bay leaves | Citrus, fruity, peppery, resinous | Most abundant hop oil (20-70% of total); highly volatile, largely lost in boil |
| **Humulene** (α-caryophyllene) | Cannabis, sage | Herbal, woody, spicy, "noble" character | Dominant in European noble hops; relatively stable |
| **Caryophyllene** (β-caryophyllene) | Rosemary, black pepper, cloves | Earthy, spicy, woody, peppery | Often co-occurs with humulene |
| **Farnesene** | Apple skin, chamomile | Herbal, grassy, green apple, citrus | Characteristic of noble and English hops; indicator of noble character |

**Key oxygenated compounds (terpenoids):**

| Compound | Found in | Flavor/Aroma | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Linalool** | Lavender, coriander | Floral, citrus, candy-like | Key marker of hop aroma in finished beer; survives fermentation better than most oils; proposed by some researchers as the best single predictor of "hoppy" aroma (debated) |
| **Geraniol** | Rose, geranium | Floral, rose-like, citrus | Biotransformed by yeast into citronellol (more potent, lime-like) |
| **Citronellol** | Citronella grass | Lime, citrus | Created during fermentation from geraniol; lower sensory threshold than geraniol |
| **Nerol** | Neroli oil | Floral, fresh, citrus | Contributes to complex citrus character |
| **Cis-rose oxide** | Rose | Fruity, floral, herbal | Found in Centennial; may explain its unique character |

**Oil content by hop type:**
- Low oil (<1.0 mL/100g): Noble hops, traditional English aroma hops
- Medium oil (1.0-2.0 mL/100g): Most dual-purpose hops
- High oil (2.0-4.0 mL/100g): American and Australian aroma bombs (Citra, Galaxy, Mosaic)
- Very high oil (>4.0 mL/100g): Rare; Polaris (4-5 mL), Galaxy (3-5 mL)

### Thiols

Sulfur-containing organic compounds (mercaptans) present in hop essential oils. Responsible for intensely tropical, passion fruit, and black currant aromas at minuscule detection thresholds (parts per trillion).

**Key thiols:**
- **4MMP** (4-methyl-4-mercaptopentanone): Black currant, catty, box tree
- **3MH** (3-mercaptohexanol): Grapefruit, passion fruit
- **3MHA** (3-mercaptohexyl acetate): Passion fruit, guava

**Terroir effect on thiols**: US-grown Perle and Nugget contain 4MMP; German-grown versions do not. The difference may relate to copper sulfate used in European vineyard/hop soils — copper binds thiols, reducing their presence.

Thiol precursors in hops are released by yeast enzymatic activity during fermentation — another mechanism behind biotransformation (see §8).

### Polyphenols and Tannins

Polyphenols comprise 3-5% of cone weight, concentrated in the green matter (bracts, leaves, stems) rather than lupulin.

**Effects:**
- Low levels: Contribute body, mouthfeel, antioxidant protection
- Moderate levels: Promote protein precipitation → chill haze, then permanent haze
- Excessive levels (from large/repeated dry hop additions): Cause **hop burn** — unpleasant astringent mouthfeel that puckers the mouth and can constrict the throat; described as a "green" astringency

**Xanthohumol**: A prenylated flavonoid found in hops, studied for antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiviral properties. Hallertau Taurus contains unusually high concentrations.

## §3 Hop Products and Processing

### Fresh/Wet Hops

Unprocessed cones used within 24-36 hours of harvest. ~80% moisture content. Require 4-6× the weight of dried hops for equivalent bittering. Produce a unique fresh, green, chlorophyll-forward character impossible to replicate with processed hops. Only available during harvest season.

### Whole Leaf (Dried)

Cones kilned within hours of harvest to reduce moisture from ~80% to 8-10%, then pressed into bales. Traditional form, still preferred for hop backs, some dry hopping, and aged/lambic hops.

**Advantages**: Natural filter bed, less vegetal matter in contact with beer
**Disadvantages**: Bulky, oxidize faster once package is opened, less surface area extraction

### T-90 Pellets

The standard commercial form. Dried hops are milled and compressed into pellets at room temperature. Named because 90% of the original green matter is retained (10% lost as dust/debris during processing).

**Advantages**: Compact storage, consistent alpha, better shelf life than cones, higher extraction efficiency
**Disadvantages**: Lose some delicate aroma compounds during processing

### T-45 Pellets (Lupulin-Enriched)

Processed at cryogenic temperatures (~-30°C / -35°C). The cold makes lupulin glands brittle and separable from green matter. Result: 45% green matter, 55% concentrated lupulin. Roughly double the alpha acid and oil content per gram vs T-90.

**Usage**: Use approximately half the weight of T-90 for equivalent contribution. Particularly valuable for dry hopping — concentrated lupulin with reduced polyphenol/tannin load means less hop burn and less vegetal character.

### Cryo Hops

A branded form of lupulin-enriched pellets (Yakima Chief Hops trademark). Similar to T-45 concept — cryogenic separation concentrating lupulin while removing green matter. Ekuanot is specifically noted for Cryo Hop production.

### CO₂ Extract

Supercritical or liquid CO₂ strips alpha acids and oils from hops. Produces a thick, paste-like concentrate.

**Types:**
- **Supercritical CO₂ extract**: Higher pressure/temperature; extracts both alpha acids and essential oils. Used for bittering and some aroma.
- **Liquid CO₂ extract**: Lower pressure; more selective extraction of specific fractions.

**Advantages**: Precise dosing, long shelf life, no vegetal matter, consistent bitterness
**Disadvantages**: Expensive, limited aroma complexity vs whole hops

### Advanced Hop Products

| Product | Description | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Isomerized pellets** | Pre-isomerized alpha acids in pellet form | Add post-boil for bitterness without boiling |
| **Iso-alpha extract** | Pure isomerized alpha acids | Precise post-fermentation bitterness adjustment |
| **Rho iso-alpha extract** | Reduced iso-alpha acids | Foam-positive bitterness, light-stable |
| **Tetra-iso extract** | Tetrahydro-iso-alpha acids | Light-stable bitterness for clear/green bottles |
| **Hexa-iso extract** | Hexahydro-iso-alpha acids | Most light-stable form of hop bitterness |
| **Beta acid extract** | Concentrated lupulones | Antimicrobial; used in some light beer production |
| **Hop aroma products** | Oil fractions, Topnotes | Post-fermentation aroma addition |
| **Type 100 plugs** | Compressed whole cones in disc form | Traditional cask conditioning |

### Hop Storage Index (HSI)

Measures the rate of alpha acid degradation. Expressed as percentage of alpha acid retained after 6 months at 20°C (68°F). Varies dramatically by variety:

- **Excellent storability** (>80% retained): Galena, Nugget, Magnum, Target
- **Good storability** (70-80%): Cascade, Centennial, Columbus, Northern Brewer
- **Fair storability** (50-70%): Willamette, Saaz, Hallertau Mittelfrüh, Crystal
- **Poor storability** (<50%): Some specialty varieties

**Best practices**: Store all hops in vacuum-sealed or nitrogen-flushed bags, frozen (-18°C / 0°F). Pellets retain alpha for 5 years frozen, 2-3 years refrigerated (unopened). Opened Cascade pellets: 2 weeks at 50°F (10°C), 5 weeks at 26°F (-3°C). Whole cones oxidize 3-5× faster than pellets when unprotected.

## §4 Hopping Techniques

### Mash Hopping

Hops added directly to the mash. Produces very smooth, mild bitterness with almost no hop aroma (oils are driven off during the boil). Rarely used in modern brewing. Low utilization (~10-15%).

### First Wort Hopping (FWH)

Hops added to the kettle as wort is lautered, before the boil begins. The hops steep in hot (but not boiling) wort during the entire runoff, then through the boil.

**Research findings**: German brewing research found FWH produces a finer, more harmonious bitterness than equivalent 60-minute additions. Tasters consistently preferred FWH beers. Paradoxically, measured IBUs are slightly higher despite perceived smoothness. Recommended for 30%+ of total hop addition.

### Bittering Additions (60-90 minutes)

Traditional method: hops boiled for 60-90 minutes for maximum isomerization. Produces clean bitterness but almost all aroma compounds are volatilized.

**Utilization by boil time** (approximate, at 1.050 OG):
| Boil Time | Utilization (Tinseth) |
|---|---|
| 90 min | 0.27 |
| 60 min | 0.25 |
| 45 min | 0.21 |
| 30 min | 0.17 |
| 15 min | 0.10 |
| 5 min | 0.05 |
| 0 min (flameout) | 0.00-0.05 |

### Flavor Additions (15-30 minutes)

Balance between bitterness and aroma/flavor. Intermediate boil times preserve some oils while still isomerizing a portion of alpha acids.

### Aroma Additions (0-5 minutes)

Late additions preserve volatile oils. Flameout/knockout additions (at flame-off, steeping 10-20 minutes before chilling) have become standard for aromatic beers.

### Whirlpool / Hopstand

Hops added after flame-out, held at elevated temperatures (typically 170-200°F / 77-93°C) for 15-60 minutes before chilling.

**Utilization**: 15-22% at whirlpool temperatures — significant bitterness contribution often overlooked in recipe formulation. Below 180°F (82°C), isomerization is negligible (the "sub iso" threshold).

**Temperature effect**: Higher whirlpool temps → more bitterness, less aroma. Lower temps → less bitterness, more aroma retention. Many brewers target 170°F (77°C) for maximum aroma with minimal bitterness.

### Hop Back

A sealed chamber filled with whole hops through which hot wort flows on the way to the chiller. Provides aroma extraction with very brief contact time at high temperature. Produces clean, bright hop flavor.

### Continuous Hopping

Small additions throughout the entire boil (e.g., every 5 minutes). Pioneered by Dogfish Head for their 60-Minute and 90-Minute IPAs. Produces a complex, layered bitterness and aroma profile.

### Hop Bursting

All hop additions concentrated in the final 10-20 minutes of the boil (no 60-minute addition). Produces intense flavor and aroma with softer perceived bitterness despite potentially high IBUs.

### Randall (Organoleptic Hop Transducer Module)

An inline device invented by Dogfish Head: beer flows from the tap through a chamber packed with fresh hops (or other ingredients) and directly into the glass. Real-time hop infusion. Primarily a draft-service novelty.

## §5 Dry Hopping

### Fundamentals

Dry hopping adds hops to beer during or after fermentation to extract aroma and flavor without isomerization (no bitterness contribution). The dominant technique for hop-forward modern styles (IPA, NEIPA, Pale Ale).

### Key Variables

**Form:**
Pellets produce more intense aroma extraction than whole cones due to ruptured lupulin glands and greater surface area. T-45/Cryo pellets deliver concentrated lupulin with reduced polyphenol load — ideal for minimizing hop burn in heavily hopped beers.

**Temperature:**
| Temperature | Effect |
|---|---|
| 32-40°F (0-4°C) | Slow extraction, cleaner character, less biotransformation |
| 50-60°F (10-16°C) | Moderate extraction, good balance |
| 60-70°F (16-21°C) | Faster extraction, more biotransformation with active yeast |
| 70-77°F (21-25°C) | Rapid extraction, maximum biotransformation, increased polyphenol pickup |

**Quantity:**
- Light hop character: 0.25-0.5 oz/gal (2-4 g/L)
- Moderate: 0.5-1.0 oz/gal (4-8 g/L)
- Heavy (IPA): 1.0-2.0 oz/gal (8-16 g/L)
- Extreme (DIPA/NEIPA): 2.0-4.0 oz/gal (16-32 g/L)
- Commercial scale: 0.5-1.5 lb/bbl standard for IPAs

**Contact time:**
- Minimum: 2-3 days for noticeable effect
- Standard: 3-5 days (most extraction occurs in first 72 hours)
- Extended: 7-14 days (diminishing returns; grassy/vegetal risk increases after 7 days)
- Maximum practical benefit reached by ~5 days for pellets, ~7 days for whole cones

### Dry Hop Timing

**Post-fermentation dry hopping** (traditional): Add after fermentation completes, during cold conditioning. Produces clean, bright hop character without yeast interaction.

**Active fermentation dry hopping** (biotransformation): Add while yeast is still active (often at 50-75% attenuation). Yeast enzymatically transforms hop oils into new aromatic compounds (see §8). Standard for NEIPA production.

**Double dry hopping (DDH)**: Two separate dry hop additions — typically one during active fermentation (for biotransformation) and one post-fermentation (for bright aroma). Coined by breweries like Trillium and Tree House.

### Hop Creep

An unwanted secondary fermentation caused by enzymes released from hop green matter during dry hopping. These diastatic enzymes (limit dextrinase, amyloglucosidase) convert residual dextrins into fermentable sugars, which yeast then ferments.

**Consequences:**
- Unintended increase in ABV (0.5-1.5% in extreme cases)
- Over-attenuation (thin body)
- Off-flavors (diacetyl from renewed yeast activity)
- Dangerous CO₂ pressure buildup in packaged beer (can cause exploding cans/bottles)

**Mitigation:**
- Use lupulin-enriched products (T-45, Cryo) — less green matter = fewer enzymes
- Shorter contact time (remove hops after 3-5 days)
- Cold dry hop (enzyme activity slows below 50°F)
- Monitor gravity after dry hopping before packaging
- Centrifuge or crash cool to remove hop material promptly (Burial Beer method)
- Package only after gravity is stable for 48+ hours post-dry-hop

### Hop Burn

Astringent, mouth-puckering sensation from excessive polyphenol extraction during dry hopping. Most common with multiple large dry hop additions using T-90 pellets or whole cones.

**Prevention:**
- Use T-45/Cryo pellets (reduced polyphenol content)
- Limit total dry hop contact time
- Avoid water chemistry that promotes polyphenol extraction (high pH, high sulfate)
- Keep total dry hop quantity reasonable (even NEIPAs rarely exceed 4 oz/gal total across all additions)

## §6 IBU Science

### Calculation Methods

**Tinseth formula** (most widely used):
```
IBU = (W × AA × U × 1000) / V

W = weight of hops (grams)
AA = alpha acid percentage (decimal)
U = utilization factor (see table below)
V = volume of wort (liters)

Utilization = f(time) × f(gravity)
f(time) = (1 - e^(-0.04 × t)) / 4.15
f(gravity) = 1.65 × 0.000125^(OG - 1)
```

**Rager formula**: Generally produces higher IBU estimates than Tinseth; applies a gravity adjustment above 1.050. Common in older brewing software.

**Garetz formula**: Most conservative estimates; accounts for bag factor, yeast absorption, and other real-world losses. Rarely used in homebrew software.

### Utilization Factors

Utilization is affected by:
- **Boil time**: Primary factor (see §4 table)
- **Wort gravity**: Higher gravity → lower utilization (sugars impede extraction). Drop ~5% utilization per 0.010 SG above 1.050
- **Hop form**: Pellets ~10-15% better utilization than whole cones (pre-ruptured lupulin)
- **Kettle geometry**: Tall, narrow vessels → better convection → slightly higher utilization
- **Temperature**: Below boiling, utilization drops sharply
- **pH**: Higher pH → slightly higher utilization
- **Humulone composition**: Higher cohumulone → slightly faster isomerization

### Perception vs. Measurement

IBU is a laboratory measurement of iso-alpha acid concentration (by spectrophotometry). It does **not** perfectly correlate with perceived bitterness:
- Above ~50-60 mg/L iso-alpha acids, perceived bitterness plateaus — 100 IBU does not taste twice as bitter as 50 IBU
- Non-iso-alpha hop material (polyphenols, oxidized beta acids) contributes to measured IBU without the same bitterness character
- Malt sweetness, body, alcohol, carbonation, and sulfate:chloride ratio all modulate bitterness perception
- The **BU:GU ratio** (IBU ÷ OG last two digits) approximates perceived bitterness balance: <0.5 malty, 0.5-0.8 balanced, >0.8 bitter

### SBU Method

Solid-phase extraction (SPE) can separate iso-alpha acids from non-iso bitter compounds, providing a more accurate "Sensory Bitterness Unit" measurement. Not widely available to homebrewers but used in quality labs.

### The Four Noble Hops

The classic European aroma hops, prized for spicy, herbal, floral character with low alpha acids. All "Saazer-type" hops:

1. **Hallertauer Mittelfrüh** (Bavaria, Germany)
2. **Tettnanger** (Baden-Württemberg, Germany)
3. **Spalt** (Nuremberg, Germany)
4. **Saaz** (Žatec, Czech Republic)

Noble hop character markers: high humulene, high farnesene, low myrcene, low cohumulone, low alpha acid (2-5%), restrained total oil.

### The "American C Hops"

The classic American craft beer varieties: **Cascade, Centennial, Chinook, Columbus (CTZ)**, and the modern addition: **Citra**.

## §8 Advanced Techniques

### Biotransformation

The enzymatic interaction between actively fermenting yeast and hop compounds that creates new flavor molecules not present in either hops or wort alone.

**Mechanisms:**
- Yeast enzymes cleave glycoside-bound hop compounds, releasing previously undetectable aroma molecules
- Geraniol is converted to β-citronellol (lower detection threshold = more aromatic per molecule)
- Thiol precursors are released and modified by yeast enzymatic activity
- Yeast metabolic byproducts interact with hop oils to form new esters and terpenoids

**Practical application:**
- Dry hop during active fermentation (50-75% apparent attenuation)
- Use healthy, vigorous yeast at sufficient pitch rate
- Temperature: 65-72°F (18-22°C) — warm enough for active yeast metabolism
- Contact time: 3-7 days during fermentation
- Pioneered by Parish Brewing, who discovered that yeast creates "awesome juicy and fruity components from the hops through a kind of refermentation if the conditions were right"

### Parish Brewing "Sub Iso" Technique

Massive doses of hops and extracts added to wort at temperatures below the isomerization threshold (~below 180-190°F / 82-88°C) in an intermediate vessel between the kettle and fermenter. This maximizes hop aroma extraction while contributing negligible bitterness.

### Sierra Nevada "Hop Torpedo"

An external column device through which beer circulates from the fermenter, flowing through a packed bed of hops and back. Adjusting circulation time, temperature, and speed controls the aroma/flavor profile. Provides intense hop character without adding hops directly to the fermenter.

### Russian River "Guth Tank Mixer"

A retractable arm with impeller inserted through a ball valve on the fermenter. Mechanically mixes tank contents after dry hopping, significantly increasing aroma extraction efficiency. Adapted from wine industry standard practice.

### Cold IPA Dry-Hop Spund (Wayfinder Beer)

Fresh, actively fermenting beer is added to a finished beer tank containing dry hops. The active yeast:
1. Biotransforms hop compounds for new flavors
2. Scrubs dissolved oxygen introduced during dry hop addition
3. Naturally carbonates the beer (spunding = capturing CO₂ under pressure)

### Centrifuge Separation (Burial Beer)

Centrifuge rapidly separates hop particulate and yeast from beer post-dry-hop. Prevents hop creep (removes enzyme source), eliminates "mouthfeel-murdering particulate," and stabilizes the beer faster than cold crashing.

### Aged Hops for Spontaneous Fermentation

Whole-cone, low-alpha hops aged minimum 2 years in a hot environment (100°F+ / 38°C+). Stored in burlap bags. Character progression:
- Fresh: green, floral, citrus
- 6-12 months: cheesy, funky
- 18+ months: earthy, hay-like, musty
- 2+ years: dry, brittle, disintegrating

Used at 0.75-1.0 lb/bbl for antimicrobial protection in spontaneous fermentation (lambic, coolship ales), typically boiled in the last 2 hours of a 3-4 hour boil. The residual beta acids and polyphenols provide selective antimicrobial activity without significant bitterness. Standard practice at Jester King, Cantillon, and other lambic-inspired breweries.

## §9 Skunking (Lightstruck Flavor)

### Chemistry

Exposure to light at wavelengths 350-500nm (UV through blue/green visible light) causes photodecomposition of isomerized alpha acids (iso-humulones). The riboflavin in beer acts as a photosensitizer, catalyzing a reaction that cleaves the iso-alpha acid molecule to produce **3-methyl-2-butene-1-thiol (MBT)** — a sulfur compound (mercaptan) with an extremely low detection threshold.

MBT is chemically identical to one of the compounds in skunk spray, hence "skunked beer."

### Prevention

| Glass Color | UV Protection | Skunking Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Brown/amber | Blocks ~98% of harmful wavelengths | Very low |
| Green | Blocks ~20% | High |
| Clear | Blocks ~0% | Very high |
| Can (aluminum) | Blocks 100% | None |

**Other protection methods:**
- **Reduced iso-alpha extracts** (tetra-iso, hexa-iso): Light-stable; used in Corona, Miller High Life, Newcastle
- **Dark storage and handling**: Keep beer away from fluorescent and sun light
- **Opaque packaging**: Cans, boxes, kegs

**Note**: Jeffrey Stuffings of Jester King intentionally uses green glass for some Farmhouse ales to induce a mild "Euro-skunk" character, demonstrating that controlled lightstruck flavor is occasionally a deliberate stylistic choice.

## §10 Terroir, Harvest, and Growing Regions

### Terroir Effects

The same hop variety grown in different regions produces detectably different character:
- **Cascade**: US-grown = classic citrus; Australian-grown = more pronounced grapefruit
- **Perle/Nugget**: US-grown contain detectable 4MMP (tropical thiol); German-grown do not (possibly due to copper sulfate in European soils binding thiol precursors)
- **Hallertau Mittelfrüh**: Grown in Hallertau district for 2000+ years; attempts to grow elsewhere produce inferior aroma despite genetic identity

### Harvest Timing

Picking window dramatically affects character:
- **Amarillo**: Early harvest = brighter, more fruity/floral; late harvest = more subdued
- **El Dorado**: Early = citrus; mid = watermelon and pear; peak maturity = pineapple, mango, stone fruit, candy lemon and cherry
- **Simcoe**: 10-day picking window. Early = grapefruit; mid = pine; late = pungent/dank (per Vinnie Cilurzo of Russian River)
- **Mosaic**: If picked too late, excess oil production leads to undesirable onion/garlic flavors

### Major Growing Regions

**United States** (~80% from Yakima Valley, WA):
- Yakima Valley, Washington: First hops in 1870s; surpassed Oregon in 1940s; now ~80% of all US production (60,000+ acres, 110+ million lbs annually)
- Willamette Valley, Oregon: Hops since 1850s; once the largest US region
- Idaho: Growing region for specialty varieties (Idaho 7)
- Michigan, New York: Historical significance; NY was hub of US production until downy mildew and Prohibition devastated it

**Germany** (Hallertau, Tettnang, Spalt, Elbe-Saale):
- Hallertau: Over two millennia of cultivation; first hop gardens date to 8th century. The Stiglmaier farm has grown hops for 180+ years (six generations)

**United Kingdom** (Kent, Herefordshire, Worcestershire):
- Flemish settlers brought hops in early 1500s; initially controversial ("Beer" with hops vs "Ale" without)
- East Kent Goldings received PDO status in 2013

**Czech Republic** (Žatec/Saaz region):
- Home of the Saaz hop; defining character of Bohemian Pilsner

**New Zealand** (Nelson, Motueka):
- Introduced by English/German immigrants mid-1800s
- Geographic isolation provides freedom from typical pests and diseases
- Breeding program produces unique proprietary varieties (Nelson Sauvin, Motueka, Riwaka)

**Australia** (Tasmania, Victoria):
- William Shoobridge brought hops from England to Tasmania in 1822
- Hop Products Australia (HPA) breeds varieties like Galaxy, Ella, Eclipse, Vic Secret, Topaz

### Industry Scale

- Over 200 million pounds of hops produced annually in 50+ countries
- 22,000+ breweries worldwide; 98% of US breweries are classified as craft
- Citra accounts for ~20% of total US hop production — the most cultivated variety
- Cluster once accounted for >90% of all US production (end of Prohibition through 1970s)
- Pride of Ringwood once accounted for 90% of Australia's total production
- Herkules is the most widely grown variety in Germany today

## §12 Breeding Programs and History

### Key Breeding Programs

**Wye College, Kent, England** (1906-present):
Only four breeders since inception: Professor E.S. Salmon (1906-53), Dr. Ray Neve (1953-81), Dr. Peter Darby (1981-2021), Klara Hajdu (2021-present). Produced Brewer's Gold and Northern Brewer in the 1930s — the "godmothers" of all higher-alpha varieties worldwide.

**USDA/Oregon State University** (1960s-present):
Developed Cascade (1960s, released 1972), Centennial (1974, released 1990), Nugget, Crystal, Liberty, Sterling, Willamette, and many others. Chuck Zimmerman and Al Haunold were key breeders.

**Hop Breeding Company, Yakima** (private):
Founded by John I. Haas and Select Botanicals Group. Developed Citra (crossed 1992 by Eugene Probasco and Jason Perrault), Mosaic, Ekuanot, Sabro, Talus, HBC 682, and experimental numbered varieties. Citra was cofunded for trial by Widmer Brothers, Sierra Nevada, and Deschutes (2007).

**Hop Research Center Hüll, Germany** (public):
Developed Hallertau Blanc, Mandarina Bavaria, Herkules, Polaris, Saphir, Hallertau Taurus, and many German dual-purpose and aroma varieties.

**Hop Products Australia (HPA)**:
Developed Galaxy (crossed 1994, released 2009), Ella, Eclipse, Vic Secret, Topaz, Super Pride. Galaxy = German Perle × high-alpha Australian cultivar.

**NZ Institute for Plant and Food Research**:
Developed Nelson Sauvin (released 2000), Motueka, Riwaka, Kohatu, Wai-iti, Waimea, Wakatu, Rakau, Pacific Sunrise. Many are triploid (three-parent) varieties.

## §14 Classification System

The Hieronymus classification system (from *For The Love of Hops*) categorizes hop varieties by flavor profile using letter codes:

| Code | Category | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | Bittering | Magnum, Warrior, Bravo, Galena |
| **N** | Noble/Traditional | Saaz, Hallertau, Tettnanger, Spalt |
| **O** | Other/Unique | Nelson Sauvin, Sorachi Ace |
| **P** | Punchy/American | Citra, Mosaic, Simcoe, Cascade |
| **S** | Spicy | Northern Brewer, Perle, Crystal |
| **T** | Tropical/New World | Galaxy, Vic Secret, El Dorado |

Most modern dual-purpose hops span multiple categories (e.g., Centennial = P/S, Mosaic = P/T).
