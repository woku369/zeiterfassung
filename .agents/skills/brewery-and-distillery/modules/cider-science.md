# Cider and Perry Science

## 1. Overview

Cider is fermented apple juice — one of humanity's oldest alcoholic beverages, predating both beer and grape wine in northern European regions where apples grew but grapes did not. Unlike beer, cider requires no malting, mashing, or boiling; unlike wine, cider apples contribute significant tannin structure alongside acidity.

**Key characteristics**:
- ABV: Typically 4–8.5% (up to 12% for apple wine; 7–13% for ice cider)
- Base: 100% apple juice (or blended with pear for "pyder")
- Fermentation: Simple — juice ferments directly with yeast, no cooking required
- Flavor drivers: Apple variety, tannin-acid balance, yeast, fermentation temperature, MLF

**Cider vs. apple wine**: Cider is made from pressed juice fermented to its natural gravity (typically 1.045–1.065). Apple wine adds sugar to boost gravity above natural levels, reaching 10–14% ABV.

## 2. Apple Science

### The Four-Quadrant Classification

Cider apples are classified by two axes: **acid** (malic acid content) and **tannin** (polyphenol content).

| Category | Acid | Tannin | Character | Role in blend |
|----------|------|--------|-----------|---------------|
| **Sweet** | < 0.45% | < 0.20% | Low acid, low tannin; mild, fruity | Body, sugar, alcohol |
| **Sharp** | > 0.45% | < 0.20% | High acid, low tannin; tart, crisp | Acidity, brightness |
| **Bittersweet** | < 0.45% | > 0.20% | Low acid, high tannin; astringent, full | Tannin, body, complexity |
| **Bittersharp** | > 0.45% | > 0.20% | High acid, high tannin; intense | Backbone, structure |

### Cider Apple Varieties

**English cider apples** (traditional bittersweets and bittersharps):

| Variety | Category | Character |
|---------|----------|-----------|
| **Kingston Black** | Bittersharp | "The king of cider apples" — can make excellent single-varietal cider; also a good eating apple |
| **Dabinett** | Bittersweet | Full-bodied, low acid; frequently used in single-varietal ciders |
| **Yarlington Mill** | Bittersweet | Medium tannin, good body; reliable cropper |
| **Tremlett's Bitter** | Bitter | Very high tannin; used for blending |
| **Redstreak** | Bittersharp | "Gingery, lemony cider"; one of the best |
| **Michelin** | Bittersweet | French origin; medium tannin, good base apple |
| **Stoke Red** | Bittersharp | Full vintage character |
| **Foxwhelp** | Bittersharp | Very strong flavor; best blended |
| **Brown Snout** | Bittersweet | Mild, fruity |
| **Harry Masters Jersey** | Bittersweet | Classic West Country; rich, full tannin |

**American cider apples**:

| Variety | Category | Notes |
|---------|----------|-------|
| **Harrison** | Bittersweet | Historic New Jersey variety; once the premier American cider apple |
| **Hewes Crab** | Bittersharp | Virginia heritage; small, intensely flavored |
| **Newtown Pippin** | Sharp | Historic; Thomas Jefferson's favorite |
| **GoldRush** | Sharp | Modern disease-resistant; high acid, good sugar |
| **Wickson Crab** | Bittersharp | Very high sugar (25° Brix); intense |

**Dessert apples used for cider** (not ideal, but common for home cidermakers):

| Variety | Character for cider |
|---------|-------------------|
| **Bramley** | Light-bodied, fairly acid; "reminiscent of an Italian soave" |
| **Cox's Orange Pippin** | Fuller body; good aromatics |
| **Russets** | Good sparkling cider; nutty character |
| **Granny Smith** | High acid; sharp, clean |
| **Gala, Golden Delicious, Braeburn** | NOT recommended — "plenty of alcohol but far too much acid and not nearly enough tannin" |

### Blending Principles

Cidermakers typically blend six or more varieties to achieve balance. The classic formula:

- **50–60%** bittersweet (body, tannin)
- **20–30%** sharp or bittersharp (acidity, backbone)
- **10–20%** sweet (sugar, aromatics)

**When using dessert apples only**: Add 0.5 kg (1 lb) of **crab apples or wildings** per 7 kg (15 lb) of dessert apples to supply tannin and acid. Alternatively, add strong black tea (1 cup per gallon) for tannin, though tea-derived tannin lacks the preservative acid component of apple tannin.

**Tannin sources** (when lacking true cider apples):
- Crab apples or wildings (best — supplies both tannin and acid)
- Strong black tea (tannin only, not acid)
- Powdered grape tannin from homebrew shops
- Oak chips (adds wood tannin character)

## 3. Juice Preparation

### Fruit Selection and Handling

**Yield**: 6–7 kg (12–15 lb) of apples produces ~5 L (1 UK gallon) of juice. Pick 8–10 kg (16–20 lb) to have reserve juice for topping up during fermentation.

**Pre-milling rest**: After picking, leave apples for **one week** at ambient temperature. This softens the cellulose fiber, improving juice extraction. French cidermakers call this *cuvage*.

**Condition**: Use sound fruit. A few blemishes are fine, but extensively rotten apples introduce acetobacter and lactic acid bacteria. Cut out any rotten sections.

### Milling

**Scratter-mill**: A hopper with vertical blades turned by hand crank or electric motor. The standard tool for cider production — reduces apples to a coarse pulp (pomace) that presses efficiently. Miniature versions available from homebrew suppliers.

**Alternatives**: Food processor (pulse to chunks, not purée), freeze-and-thaw (breaks cell walls), or hand-chop and pound in a bucket. Blenders and juicers process only small batches and may not save time.

### Pressing

Use a **screw press** or **hydraulic press** to extract juice from pomace.

**Technique**: Take a few turns at a time. Wait for the rush of juice to subside, then take a few more turns. Even when the pomace seems completely dry, resting and pressing again yields additional juice. **Rehydrate** pressed-dry pomace with a small amount of water and press a second time to extract the last fermentable sugar.

**Metal contact warning**: Only stainless steel or aluminum should contact juice. Copper and zinc produce taints that may be toxic. Iron causes blackening (especially problematic for perry).

### Pre-Fermentation Treatment

**Sulfite addition**: 1 crushed Campden tablet per 5 L (1 gallon), or 50 ppm SO₂. Seal airtight for 24 hours. This suppresses wild yeasts and bacteria (including acetobacter) while leaving the juice receptive to cultured yeast pitched after the 24-hour waiting period.

**Wild/spontaneous fermentation**: Skip sulfite if you want native yeasts to conduct fermentation (see §6 Regional Styles — French and English traditional).

**Pectic enzyme**: Optional. Pectin causes haze in cider; pectic enzyme breaks it down for a clear product. Add per packet instructions at the same time as sulfite. Many traditional cidermakers prefer cloudy cider and skip this.

## 4. Fermentation Science

### Juice Chemistry

| Parameter | Typical range | Notes |
|-----------|--------------|-------|
| **Specific gravity** | 1.040–1.065 | Dessert apples: 1.045–1.060; cider apples: 1.050–1.065 |
| **Brix** | 10–16° | Corresponds to SG range above |
| **pH** | 3.2–4.0 | Target: 3.3–3.8 for balanced cider |
| **Titratable acidity** | 3–7 g/L malic acid | Sharp apples: 6–9 g/L; sweet: 1–3 g/L |
| **Tannin** | 0.01–0.40% | Dessert: 0.01–0.10%; cider apples: 0.10–0.40% |
| **YAN** | 9–249 mg N/L | Average ~59 mg N/L; 94% of samples below 140 mg N/L |

If juice gravity is below 1.055, add dissolved sugar: **70 g (2.5 oz) per gallon raises gravity by 5 points**.

### Yeast Selection

| Yeast | Type | Temp range | Character |
|-------|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Wild/native** | Mixed flora | 4–24°C | Complex, funky, variable; traditional English/French |
| **Lalvin EC-1118** | Champagne | 7–35°C | Clean, neutral, high alcohol tolerance; reliable workhorse |
| **Lalvin 71B** | Wine | 15–30°C | Metabolizes 20–40% of malic acid; softens sharp ciders |
| **Mangrove Jack's M02** | Cider-specific | 12–25°C | Developed for cider; preserves fruit character |
| **Fermentis SafCider** | Cider-specific | 10–30°C | Multiple variants (AC-4, AS-2, TF-6); French cider trials |
| **Nottingham Ale** | Ale | 14–21°C | Neutral; some cidermakers prefer the slight residual body |
| **Belle Saison** | Belgian | 15–35°C | Phenolic, fruity esters; unconventional but interesting |

**Champagne yeast** is the most commonly recommended for beginners — clean, reliable, and tolerant of the low-nutrient, low-pH cider environment.

### Yeast Nutrients

Apple juice is chronically deficient in **yeast assimilable nitrogen (YAN)**. Average apple juice YAN is ~59 mg N/L; healthy fermentation requires a minimum of **140–200 mg N/L**.

**Consequences of low YAN**: Stuck/sluggish fermentation, hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) production (rotten egg/cabbage smell), poor yeast health.

| Nutrient product | YAN contribution (at 1 g/gal) | Character |
|-----------------|-------------------------------|-----------|
| **DAP** (diammonium phosphate) | ~50 ppm | Inorganic nitrogen; cheap, effective, but can promote off-aromas if over-used |
| **Fermaid K** | ~25 ppm | Blended organic/inorganic; includes vitamins, minerals |
| **Fermaid O** | ~10 ppm | 100% organic (yeast derivatives); best for aroma development; 3–4x more efficiently used than inorganic nitrogen |
| **GoFerm** | Rehydration nutrient | Used during yeast rehydration, not as primary YAN source |

**Timing**: Add nutrients in two stages — at inoculation and at **1/3 sugar depletion** (when ~1/3 of gravity drop has occurred). This avoids early YAN depletion that causes mid-fermentation stress.

**Historical nitrogen sources**: Traditional cidermakers discovered that throwing meat (bacon, or "quite possibly rat") into a stuck vat would restart fermentation — the decomposing protein released nitrogen the yeast needed.

### Fermentation Temperature

| Style | Temperature | Duration | Notes |
|-------|------------|----------|-------|
| **Standard/home** | 15–22°C (59–72°F) | 2–4 weeks | Most common range |
| **English traditional** | 10–15°C (50–59°F) | 1–3 months | Slow, cool; retains volatile aromatics |
| **French traditional** | 12–15°C (53–59°F) | 2–4 months | Very slow; essential for keeved cider |
| **Wild fermentation** | 4–24°C (40–75°F) | Weeks to months | Optimal ~15°C; wild yeasts work down to 4°C |
| **Cold fermentation** | 5–12°C (40–53°F) | Months | Complex flavors; requires patient strains |
| **Warm fermentation** | 20–25°C (68–77°F) | 1–2 weeks | Faster but risks fusel alcohols, lost aromatics |

**Key principle**: Cooler fermentation preserves volatile fruit aromatics; warmer fermentation drives them off with escaping CO₂. Aim for gravity to fall ~1 degree per day. If faster, move to a cooler location; if slower, insulate the fermenter.

**Do not exceed 25°C** — produces fusel alcohols and "pear drop" (ethyl acetate) off-flavors. Above 35°C, most yeast strains deteriorate.

### Malo-Lactic Fermentation (MLF)

After primary fermentation completes, a **bacterial fermentation** should begin naturally. MLF converts sharp malic acid into the softer lactic acid, rounding out the cider's flavor.

**Indicators**: A slow stream of bubbles reappears through the airlock after primary fermentation has ended.

**If MLF doesn't start naturally**: Purchase *Oenococcus oeni* MLF culture from homebrew shops. MLF is especially beneficial for sharp, high-acid ciders.

**Note**: MLF is desirable for most styles but some cidermakers deliberately prevent it (with sulfite) in sharp styles where bright acidity is wanted.

## 5. Keeving (French Technique)

Keeving (*défécation*) is the traditional French method for producing naturally sweet, low-alcohol cider without pasteurization or chemical stabilization. It works by deliberately **nutrient-starving** the yeast.

### Process

1. **Mill** apples as normal
2. **Hold pomace** for **48 hours** before pressing (maceration — develops pectin and flavor)
3. **Press** and collect juice
4. **Add calcium chloride**: 1 mL calcium chloride solution per 10 L (2 gallons) of must, added the day after pressing
5. **Wait for separation**: The must separates into three layers:
   - Bottom: sediment
   - Middle: clear juice
   - Top: **pectin gel** (chapeau brun / "brown hat") — this gel traps nitrogen, thiamine, and proteins the yeast needs
6. **Siphon** the clear middle layer into an open fermenter
7. **Skim brown caps**: A thick brown cap forms on the surface; skim it off. If another forms, skim again
8. **Transfer** to demijohn with airlock once caps stop forming
9. **Fermentation stalls naturally**: The nutrient-deprived yeast exhausts itself, leaving the cider at FG ~1.025–1.030 with residual sweetness and moderate alcohol

### Requirements for Successful Keeving

- **High-pectin apples** (most cider varieties; not modern dessert cultivars)
- **Low-nitrogen fruit** (under-fertilized orchards produce lower-nitrogen apples, ideal for keeving)
- **Cool temperatures** (10–15°C) — essential for controlled, slow fermentation
- **No nutrient additions** — the entire point is nutrient deprivation
- **Calcium chloride** (or PME — pectin methylesterase enzyme) to promote gel formation

### Result

Sweet, gently sparkling cider at 2–4% ABV — the classic French *cidre doux*. Keeved cider can be bottle-conditioned: the residual sugar slowly ferments in bottle, producing gentle natural carbonation (pétillant).

## 6. Regional Styles

### English Cider

**West Country** (Somerset, Devon, Herefordshire, Gloucestershire): The heartland of English cider. Made from traditional bittersweet and bittersharp varieties. Character: full-bodied, tannic, often still or lightly sparkling, ranging from bone-dry to medium-sweet. Farmhouse versions are unfiltered and can be funky.

**Eastern Counties** (Kent, East Anglia): Often lighter, made with sharper dessert and culinary apples. Crisper, more acidic profile.

**Sparkling English cider**: Méthode champenoise applied to cider (champagne bottles, riddling, disgorgement). Premium product. Russets are noted as particularly suited to sparkling cider.

### French Cidre

Normandy and Brittany are the premier French cider regions.

**Classifications** (by residual sugar):

| Classification | Residual sugar | ABV | Character |
|---------------|---------------|-----|-----------|
| **Cidre doux** | > 35 g/L | 1.5–3% | Sweet, low alcohol |
| **Cidre demi-sec** | 17–35 g/L | 3–5% | Medium-sweet |
| **Cidre brut** | < 17 g/L | 4.5–6% | Dry |

French cider relies on **keeving** for natural sweetness and **wild yeast fermentation** at cool temperatures (12–15°C). The resulting ciders are characteristically complex, with farmhouse funk, low alcohol, and gentle natural carbonation.

**Pommeau**: Norman aperitif — fresh apple juice blended with apple brandy (Calvados), then aged in oak.

### Spanish Sidra

**Sidra natural** (Asturias, Basque Country): Uncarbonated, still cider with pronounced acidity and funky, sometimes acetic character. Made from local sharp and bittersharp varieties.

**Escanciado**: The traditional Asturian pouring technique — cider is poured from a bottle held high above the head into a glass held at waist level. The long pour aerates the cider, briefly creating effervescence and releasing aromatics. Served in small portions, drunk immediately.

**Sidra de nueva expresión**: Modern Spanish sparkling cider, closer to champagne method. Growing category.

### American Craft Cider

The American cider revival (2010s onward) encompasses diverse styles:

- **Heritage/orchard cider**: Traditional bittersweet/bittersharp varieties grown in New England, Virginia, Pacific Northwest
- **Modern cider**: Made from dessert apples (often store-bought juice); clean, fruit-forward, sometimes back-sweetened
- **Hopped cider**: Dry-hopped with American hop varieties; crossover appeal for beer drinkers
- **Botanical cider**: Infused with ginger, berries, herbs, spices
- **Wild/spontaneous**: Natural fermentation; funky, complex; influenced by natural wine movement
- **New England style**: Heritage varieties, often barrel-aged, tannic, still or pétillant

### Ice Cider (Cidre de Glace)

Québécois specialty (originated ~1990s). Intensely sweet, concentrated dessert cider (7–13% ABV, high residual sugar).

**Two production methods**:

1. **Cryoextraction** (more common): Apples are left on the tree or in outdoor storage through hard freezes. When pressed while frozen, the ice crystals remain behind and only the concentrated sugary juice runs out. Starting Brix: 30–40° (vs. normal 10–16°)

2. **Cryoconcentration**: Juice is pressed normally, then frozen outdoors. The frozen water is removed, leaving concentrated juice behind

**Fermentation**: Very slow (months) at cold temperatures. High sugar means the yeast struggles, leaving substantial residual sweetness. Results in a lusciously sweet, apple-essence dessert wine.

### Pétillant Naturel (Pét-Nat) Cider

The oldest method of making sparkling beverages — possibly invented by cidermakers, not winemakers.

**Method**: Bottle the cider **before primary fermentation finishes** (at ~9 g/L residual sugar). The remaining sugar ferments in the sealed bottle, producing ~2.0–2.5 volumes of CO₂. No sugar or yeast is added — the existing fermentation simply finishes in bottle.

**Characteristics**:
- Lighter, frothier carbonation than méthode champenoise
- Often cloudy (sediment from spent yeast)
- Crown-capped
- Variable effervescence and sweetness
- Lees contribute biscuit, bread dough, toast notes
- "Nothing in / Nothing out" (Zero/Zero) philosophy

## 7. Perry

Perry is cider made from **pears** — a distinct and challenging beverage, not simply "pear cider."

### Perry Pear Varieties

True perry pears are distinct from dessert pears. They are small, hard, astringent, and often inedible fresh. Key varieties:

| Variety | Character |
|---------|-----------|
| **Blakeney Red** | Classic; reliable, full-flavored |
| **Thorn** | Sharp; excellent for blending |
| **Hendre Huffcap** | Medium tannin; good single varietal |
| **Yellow Huffcap** | Sweet; adds body |
| **Moorcroft** | Bittersharp; intense |
| **Robin** (East Anglian culinary pear) | "Makes a very acceptable perry" from a non-perry variety |

**Dessert pears**: Not recommended for perry — too soft when ripe (difficult to press), vulnerable to infection, and their juice tends to blacken.

**Perry pear trees**: Can take **20+ years** to yield a worthwhile crop. Most are ancient, often centuries-old trees.

### Key Differences from Apple Cider

- **Higher tannin**: Pears contain substantially more tannin than apples. **Precipitate excess tannin** by standing juice on the pulp overnight in a cold place before racking to fermenter
- **Nitrogen deficiency**: Pears are deficient in soluble nitrogen — use **generous yeast nutrient**
- **Continuous sediment**: Perry throws sediment throughout maturation; **rack frequently**
- **Ferment cold**: Conduct fermentation at the lowest achievable temperature
- **Sorbitol**: Pears contain sorbitol, an **unfermentable sugar** that gives perry a distinctive rich, buttery mouthfeel — this is perry's defining quality
- **Blackening risk**: Perry and pear wine are particularly susceptible to enzymatic browning. Prevent with 2 Campden tablets per 5 L of must
- **pH target**: 3.9–4.0

### "Pyder" (Apple-Pear Blend)

A blend of apple and pear juice. Advantages:
- Pears supply tannin missing from dessert apples
- Sorbitol compensates for yeast's tendency toward bone dryness
- Check and reduce acidity of the blended must, as the combination can be over-acid

## 8. Carbonation and Finishing

### Still Cider

Ferment to dryness, rack, mature for several weeks, bottle in standard wine bottles. No carbonation step.

### Bottle Conditioning (Priming)

1. Ferment to dryness
2. Rack into clean vessel; mature several weeks
3. Add **1 level teaspoon of sugar** and a small yeast starter per 750 mL
4. Bottle in **champagne bottles only** — standard wine bottles cannot withstand the pressure
5. Store upright; carbonation develops over 2–4 weeks

**Warning**: Cider continues fermenting slowly even after appearing finished. Ordinary wine or beer bottles may explode months later. Always use champagne-grade glass for carbonated cider.

### Méthode Champenoise

Full champagne method: bottle conditioning, then riddling (turning bottles to settle lees into neck), freezing the neck plug, and disgorging. Produces the finest sparkling cider but labor-intensive.

### Pétillant Naturel

Bottle before primary fermentation finishes (see §6). Simplest sparkling method — no additions needed.

### Force Carbonation

Keg the finished cider and carbonate with CO₂ at 10–12 PSI at 3–5°C for 5–7 days. Most consistent results but requires kegging equipment.

### Closures

- **Crown caps**: Standard for sparkling cider; reliable seal
- **Champagne cork + wire cage**: Traditional for premium sparkling
- **Screw caps** (heavy-duty): Common among cidermakers for still or lightly sparkling
- **Swing-top bottles**: Convenient but verify pressure rating

### Storage Vessels for Draught Cider

- **Polycask**: Preferred by cidermakers — no venting valve; keeps 2–3 years unbroached. Strong West Country cider stays drinkable for 3+ months even after broaching
- **Pressure barrel**: 25 L (5 gal) plastic with tap and CO₂ vent
- **Polypin**: Collapsible bag-in-box; good air exclusion but hard to clean; short-term use, add Campden tablet

## 9. Back-Sweetening

Cider yeast typically ferments to complete dryness (FG < 1.000). For sweeter styles:

### Methods

1. **Keeving** (§5): Natural nutrient-depletion; no additives needed. Traditional but requires specific conditions

2. **Potassium sorbate + sulfite**: Add potassium sorbate (½ tsp per gallon) AND Campden tablet (1 per gallon) to prevent re-fermentation. Then add sugar/juice to taste. Sorbate alone is insufficient — it prevents yeast reproduction but doesn't kill existing yeast; sulfite is needed as a co-stabilizer

3. **Pasteurization**: Heat sealed bottles to 65°C for 10 minutes. Kills yeast but also degrades delicate cider flavors — Bruning warns "the process can ruin delicate drinks, especially cider"

4. **Sterile filtration**: Filter through 0.45 µm (or finer) membrane to remove all yeast cells. Add sweetener afterward. Requires equipment investment

5. **Non-fermentable sweeteners**: Erythritol, xylitol, stevia, or Splenda. Avoids re-fermentation risk entirely. Erythritol is often preferred for its clean taste and no cooling effect at typical cider doses

6. **Campden tablets alone**: 1 per 5 L — can temporarily halt fermentation, but the effect wears off and "your cider can suddenly start working again, with potentially explosive consequences"

## 10. Cider Faults and Troubleshooting

| Fault | Cause | Prevention / Fix |
|-------|-------|-----------------|
| **Stuck fermentation** | Low YAN, excessive CO₂, wrong temperature | Stir to release CO₂ and aerate slightly; add fresh yeast starter with nutrient; check temperature |
| **Hydrogen sulfide (rotten egg)** | YAN deficiency; stressed yeast | Add Fermaid O or DAP at inoculation and 1/3 depletion; ensure 140+ ppm YAN; rack off lees promptly |
| **Acetification (vinegar)** | Acetobacter exposure to air | Minimize headspace; top up vessels; sulfite; fill demijohns to brim. If caught early: 2 Campden tablets per 5 L |
| **Pectin haze** | Undegraded pectin | Test: mix 30 mL cider with 100 mL methylated spirits — pectin forms jelly-like clot. Fix: add pectic enzyme |
| **Film yeast** | Surface growth breaking down alcohol | 2 Campden tablets per 5 L; minimize headspace; if persistent, cover surface with olive oil |
| **Mouse taint** | *Brettanomyces* or LAB infection | Untreatable — "makes the wine undrinkable." Prevent with rigorous sanitation |
| **Lactic acid bacteria** | Infection from rotten fruit | Hazy sheen. Fix: 3 Campden tablets per 5 L |
| **Blackening/darkening** | Enzymatic oxidation or metal contact | 2 Campden tablets per 5 L of must; avoid iron/copper/zinc equipment; especially affects perry |
| **Fermentation too fast** | Temperature too high | Volatile aromatics lost to escaping CO₂. Move to cooler location |
| **Fermentation too slow** | Temperature too low; nutrient deficiency | Insulate fermenter; add nutrient; spoilage microbes may regrow before fermentation completes |
| **Pear drop / nail polish** | Ethyl acetate; fermentation above 25°C | Ferment cooler; cannot be fixed retroactively |
| **Caramel off-flavor** | Using juice concentrate | Concentrate (reduced by boiling) produces unwanted caramel, especially in cider; use fresh juice |

## 11. Equipment

### Cider-Specific

- **Scratter-mill**: Hopper with rotating blades (hand crank or electric motor). The primary cider tool — reduces apples to pressable pomace. Miniature versions available from homebrew suppliers
- **Screw press** or **hydraulic press**: Extracts juice from pomace. Essential for pure-juice cider

### Shared with Wine/Beer Making

- Fermentation vessel (demijohn, carboy, bucket with airlock)
- Siphon tube
- Hydrometer
- Thermometer
- Campden tablets, pectic enzyme, yeast nutrient
- Muslin/cheesecloth for straining
- Champagne bottles (for sparkling cider)

## 12. Practical Home Cider Making

### Basic Recipe (5 L / 1 UK gallon)

**Ingredients**:
- 8–10 kg (16–20 lb) apples — mixture of varieties (Bramleys + Coxes work well; add crab apples for tannin if using only dessert fruit)
- 1 Campden tablet (optional — omit for wild ferment)
- Yeast: champagne (EC-1118) or cider-specific strain
- Yeast nutrient (essential for garden/hedgerow fruit)
- Pectic enzyme (optional, for clear cider)
- Sugar if OG below 1.055: 70 g (2.5 oz) per gallon raises gravity by 5 points

**Process**:
1. Rest apples 1 week after picking
2. Mill in scratter or chop/crush
3. Press; rehydrate pomace and press again
4. Add Campden tablet; seal airtight 24 hours
5. Test gravity; adjust if needed
6. Add yeast nutrient and pectic enzyme
7. Pitch yeast; transfer to demijohn, fill to brim
8. Cover mouth with foil — expect vigorous foaming within 48 hours
9. Once foam subsides, rack to clean vessel; top up with reserve juice; fit airlock
10. Ferment cool (~15°C); target 1 gravity point per day
11. After 2–3 months, primary is complete; allow MLF to proceed (slow bubbles restart)
12. Rack off lees; mature; bottle still or with priming sugar in champagne bottles

### Apple Wine Variation

- 2.5–3 kg (5–6.5 lb) chopped apples steeped in water (maceration, not pressing)
- 1.75–2 kg (4–4.5 lb) sugar
- Pectic enzyme essential
- Yeast nutrient
- Makes a stronger (10–14% ABV) apple beverage — excellent carrier for spices (ginger, cinnamon, nutmeg)
